## Recreations

These sketches are **homages to artists we admire**. Each one rebuilds a piece of theirs to learn how it was made, and to see what a modern framework brings to it.

Some of these artists worked on the first computers and plotters. Many never used a computer at all. A few write software today.

The section is laid out like a small museum. The artists are grouped into **wings** by how they made their work. Each artist has a page about their life and their work, with the sketches made after it. If you are looking for someone, the full list is [at the end](#all-artists-a-to-z).

Each sketch is a homage made after the artist. It is not a reproduction of a specific work, and it is not affiliated with or endorsed by the artist or their estate.

### How the choices are made

#### Chance

These artists let chance make some of the decisions. They wrote a rule, then used a die, a coin, or a table of random numbers to fill it in. Several of them kept the throws and showed them beside the picture.

#### Arrangements

The artists in this wing start with a few parts and show the ways they can go together. The parts might be the twelve edges of a cube, a small set of shapes, or one structure and a few moves. The work is the whole series.

| [**Manuel Felguérez**](ManuelFelguerez/), 1928 to 2020. A painter who had a computer compose in his own geometric language. | [**Eduardo Terrazas**](EduardoTerrazas/), born 1936. #### Growth

The pictures in this wing grow by themselves. The artist writes a small rule for many small parts, and a program runs it thousands of times. Cracks spread into a city map, and circles leave a line wherever they meet.

|---|---|---|---|
| [**Casey Reas**](CaseyReas/), born 1972. Writes instructions for software to carry out. | [**Jared Tarbell**](JaredTarbell/), born 1973. Draws cities and sand paintings that grow from simple rules. |  |  |

### What the work is made of

#### Line

The work in this wing is made of lines. Some are ruled by hand and some are drawn by a plotter. In others, one line or circle is repeated a small step at a time until a form appears.

### Where the work happens

These works change while you look at them. Lines seem to move, and the picture changes as you walk past it. Part of the work happens in your eye.

This work is made in three dimensions. Some of it hangs from the ceiling, some is cut from a block, and some is worn. You can walk around it, and sometimes into it.

### Where the idea comes from

The idea comes from the [**Recreating the Past**](https://sfpc.io/recreatingthepast-spring2020/) class at the [School for Poetic Computation (SFPC)](https://sfpc.io), taught by Zach Lieberman. The class states its aim this way: *"By recreating historic works, we also investigate what modern tools and approaches have to offer"*. So the class treats history as something you take part in, not only something you consume.

### How these differ from the other examples

The other examples are a *feature* learning path, so each one answers the question "how do I use X?" for one feature. Recreations are organized **by artist** instead, and each one asks "how was this made, and how does it feel to rebuild it?" about one piece. They are maintained and compile-tested like every example. Each one also tends to push the framework, because a recreation that is awkward to write tells us what to build next.
