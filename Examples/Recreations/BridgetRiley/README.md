### Her life

**Bridget Riley** is a British painter and a central figure of **Op art**, a label she has always resisted. She was born in Norwood, London. During the war she lived in Cornwall with her mother and sister. School was patchy, and her mother encouraged her to look closely at water, plants, and rocks. She studied at Goldsmiths College from 1949 to 1952 and at the Royal College of Art from 1952 to 1955. Then she taught children, taught in art schools, and worked for an advertising agency.

In 1959 she copied Georges Seurat's *Bridge at Courbevoie* from a reproduction, following his thinking more than his method. She has called that copy pivotal in her change from figurative painting to abstract painting. *Movement in Squares* came in 1961. She drew it without a pause, she wrote later, and then painted every other space black. Her first solo show was at Gallery One in London in the spring of 1962.

She is best known for her black-and-white work of the 1960s, in which precise geometry produces vivid optical movement and shimmer. In 1965 her work was shown in MoMA's landmark exhibition *The Responsive Eye*, which brought Op art to international attention. A detail of *Current* was on the cover of the catalog. Her paintings were also copied onto fabrics that filled shop windows in New York, and that distressed her.

Color came by steps: grays first, colored grays from 1965, and color in its own right from 1967. In 1968 she represented Great Britain at the Venice Biennale and won its International Prize for Painting. After a trip to Egypt around 1980 she began a series of stripe paintings in colors she had seen in the tombs there. Wall paintings followed, and *Messengers* (2019) is painted straight onto a wall of the National Gallery in London.

### What she made

She works each painting out in studies first: pencil and gouache on paper, bands of painted paper cut and moved around, and then a cartoon at full size. Assistants paint the final canvas, as they have since 1961. She compares the diagrams they follow to a musical score. That is not a wish for a machine's finish. "I want my works to be perceived as made things," she has said.

The **"Fragments" (1965)** are a series of seven black-and-white screenprints on **Plexiglas**, printed at Kelpra Studio, London, and published by the Robert Fraser Gallery. She chose them from images that had turned up while she prepared paintings and had stayed, in her words, "fragments of a theme". *Fragment 3* is a field of bands bent into chevrons, and it is the one recreated here. **"Current" (1964)** is one of her best-known black-and-white paintings. The whole board is filled with closely spaced lines that run from top to bottom and undulate. The waves tighten in a band just above the middle, so the surface seems to ripple.

### Rebuilding her work

Each of these works is one line repeated across the surface with a small change from one to the next: a band bent at a new angle, or a wave whose length tightens toward the middle. A sketch states that as a rule for the line and a rule for the change. Then it can let the lines move, slowly, which the originals only seem to do.

- [**Fragment 3**](Fragment3/): a field of black-and-white chevron stripes (horizontal bands bent into a zigzag), after Riley's *Fragment 3* (1965).

- [**Current**](Current/): a field of parallel undulating lines that shimmer, after Riley's *Current* (1964).

  Each line is a smooth `drawCurve` through sampled points. The sketch is a loose reading: its lines run across the canvas and the middle ones carry the tighter waves, where the painting's lines run down the board and each tightens as it crosses a band above the middle.
