# Influences & attribution

Ollin borrows ideas from many frameworks, papers, and published techniques, and reimplements them independently. This file is the full record of those debts: the framework influences, the bundled third-party source, the engineering references, and the technique behind each helper. The short version lives in the [README](README.md#influences--attribution); the license text for every bundled component is collected in [`THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES.md`](THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES.md).

## Frameworks

Ollin builds on the ideas of three creative-coding frameworks and reimplements them in Swift. Because it does not copy their source code, none of their licenses attach to Ollin, which stays MIT:

| Project | License | What Ollin takes (influence only) |
|---|---|---|
| [p5.js](https://p5js.org) | LGPL-2.1 | Friendly, learn-it-in-an-afternoon API names and the `setup()` / `draw()` lifecycle |
| [OPENRNDR](https://openrndr.org) | BSD-2-Clause | The typed `Program` / `Drawer` core and composable geometry |
| [openFrameworks](https://openframeworks.cc) | MIT | Simple project structure and the per-example folder layout |

That `setup()` / `draw()` vocabulary started in [Processing](https://processing.org), the Java project p5.js grew out of. Ollin follows p5's spelling because that's the version most people coming to it already know.

"Inspired by" means borrowing ideas and API vocabulary, which is different from copying code; Ollin's implementation is written independently. Individual example sketches that are ported from a published source name that source, its author, and its license in the file header. Only sources whose licenses permit redistribution under MIT are used.

## Techniques

Ollin's helpers reimplement well-known published techniques, written from the paper, article, or formula and credited here, grouped by area. They lean toward OPENRNDR-style ergonomics.

### Rendering & anti-aliasing

- The signed-distance fields behind Ollin's analytic shapes (circles, ellipses, rectangles, lines, arcs, triangles, n-gons and stars, quadratic Bézier curves, point markers, and the rest of the catalog) come from [Inigo Quilez's 2D distance functions](https://iquilezles.org/articles/distfunctions2d/), including the onion operator behind rings and the `hollow`/`solid` band mode. SVG export traces these same functions on the CPU (marching squares) to turn each curved shape into a vector outline.
- The SDF combinators behind `drawSDF` and the `smoothUnion { }` block (smooth-minimum union, subtraction, and intersection, the `round`/`onion` modifiers, morph, and the per-axis `stretched` elongation) reimplement [Inigo Quilez's distance-function operators](https://iquilezles.org/articles/distfunctions/): the polynomial smooth-minimum (whose blend factor also melts the two operands' colors across the seam), the boolean operators, and the `opElongate` stretch, with the domain operators (mirror, tiled and radial repetition) and the joint and detailing operators (the chamfer, stairs, and columns trios plus engrave, groove, tongue, and pipe) after [hg_sdf](https://mercury.sexy/hg_sdf/) (used under its MIT option). The conservative non-uniform `scaled(x:y:)` multiplies the distance by the smallest scale factor (the Lipschitz bound that keeps sphere tracing safe). Cross-read against [LYGIA](https://lygia.xyz)'s `sdf` set, then written independently.
- The raymarched 3D SDF combinators behind `drawSDF3D` sphere-trace the merged field (the method formalized by John C. Hart, ["Sphere Tracing: A Geometric Method for the Antialiased Ray Tracing of Implicit Surfaces"](https://doi.org/10.1007/s003710050084), *The Visual Computer* 12, 1996) with [Inigo Quilez's raymarching of distance fields](https://iquilezles.org/articles/raymarchingdf/) (the step fudge factor that keeps a smooth-minimum or scaled field, which return distance *bounds*, from overshooting), [his 3D distance functions](https://iquilezles.org/articles/distfunctions/) for the primitives (sphere, box, rounded box, torus, capsule, cylinder, cone, octahedron, ellipsoid, infinite plane), and the four-tap tetrahedron gradient for the surface normal. The self-shadowing is [his soft-shadow penumbra march](https://iquilezles.org/articles/rmshadows/), the silhouette anti-aliasing the same closest-approach / pixel-cone measure applied to the camera ray, and the mirror, tiled, and radial (`pModPolar`) domain operators plus the joint and detailing operators (the chamfer, stairs, and columns trios, engrave, groove, tongue, and pipe) follow [hg_sdf](https://mercury.sexy/hg_sdf/) (used under its MIT option). The sculpting tier is his too: the `line`/`hexPrism`/`pyramid`/`cappedTorus`/`link` leaves are more of [his 3D distance functions](https://iquilezles.org/articles/distfunctions/), and `twisted`/`bent`/`displaced` reimplement his `opTwist`/`opCheapBend`/`opDisplace` distortions (with a conservative distance rescale, sized from the distortion's strength, keeping the sphere trace safe). The value-type and scoped API shape was studied from [ShaderPark](https://github.com/shader-park/shader-park-core) (MIT). Written independently.
- Stroked lines and curves (`drawLine`/`drawBezier`/`drawPolyline` and shape outlines) anti-alias by expanding each stroke into triangles with a feathered ~1px coverage fringe carried in the geometry, the edge-expansion approach of [Antigrain Geometry](https://agg.sourceforge.net/antigrain.com/index.html) (Maxim Shemanarev) and [NanoVG](https://github.com/memononen/nanovg) (Mikko Mononen), reimplemented independently. Consecutive segments end on their shared inner miter point rather than each on its own perpendicular, so a turn is drawn once and translucent ink lays down one coat, with a fallback to square ends where that point would fall outside a neighboring segment; that is NanoVG's inner-join model (`nvg__chooseBevel` and the inner-bevel test in `nvg__calculateJoins`), also written from the technique, and shared with the tessellated path that strokes outline-font glyphs.
- The SDF glyph atlas behind `textMode(.atlas)` follows Chris Green's signed-distance-field text method (Valve, ["Improved Alpha-Tested Magnification for Vector Textures and Special Effects"](https://steamcdn-a.akamaihd.net/apps/valve/2007/SIGGRAPH2007_AlphaTestedMagnification.pdf), SIGGRAPH 2007), with the distance fields generated by the exact separable Euclidean distance transform of [Felzenszwalb and Huttenlocher](https://cs.brown.edu/people/pfelzens/dt/) ("Distance Transforms of Sampled Functions", 2012).
- Rendering happens in linear light (blending and MSAA resolve in linear space, into sRGB-encoded targets) with a small triangular-PDF dither on the 8-bit output to ease gradient banding; the per-pixel dither value comes from Dave Hoskins' [Hash without Sine](https://www.shadertoy.com/view/4djSRW).
- The present pass's tone-map modes reimplement published curves: `.reinhard` is the global operator from Erik Reinhard, Michael Stark, Peter Shirley, and James Ferwerda, *Photographic Tone Reproduction for Digital Images* (SIGGRAPH 2002), and `.aces` is the Academy Color Encoding System's filmic response in [Krzysztof Narkowicz's fitted approximation](https://knarkowicz.wordpress.com/2016/01/06/aces-filmic-tone-mapping-curve/). Source-over compositing follows Thomas Porter and Tom Duff, *Compositing Digital Images* (SIGGRAPH 1984); the wider `blendMode` vocabulary (multiply, screen, lightest, darkest) is the image-editor tradition, expressed as fixed-function blend states.
- Wide-gamut and high-dynamic-range output (`colorOutput`) is built on published standards rather than any implementation. The gamut conversions are changes of primaries between D65 spaces, computed from the chromaticities in IEC 61966-2-1 (sRGB), SMPTE EG 432-1 / RP 431-2 (the DCI-P3 primaries Display P3 uses with the sRGB transfer curve), and ITU-R BT.2020; the perceptual-quantizer transfer function is SMPTE ST 2084 (also ITU-R BT.2100), implemented from its published equations. That an exported HDR file's diffuse white sits at 203 cd/m² is the reference level of ITU-R BT.2408. The extended-sRGB convention for components outside 0…1, which is what lets a Display P3 color ride the ordinary pipeline, follows the same treatment in Apple's extended color spaces and the CSS Color 4 predefined-space model.
- The corner-pin warp behind `Installation.Projection` is the projective mapping of a square onto a quadrilateral. It comes from Paul Heckbert's *Fundamentals of Texture Mapping and Image Warping* (master's thesis, UC Berkeley, 1989). The present pass runs it backwards, per fragment. Written from the equations.
- The edge fades that let two projectors share one wall follow Paul Bourke's ["Edge blending using commodity projectors"](https://paulbourke.net/miscellaneous/edgeblend/) (2004). His blending function is half at the middle of the shared band and flat at either end. Ollin applies the weight to linear light rather than to a code value. Written from the paper.
- The ground-grid viewport floor (`groundGrid()`) draws its level-of-detail, infinitely-receding grid with the anti-aliased screen-space-derivative method from [Ben Golus, "The Best Darn Grid Shader (Yet)"](https://bgolus.medium.com/the-best-darn-grid-shader-yet-727f9278b9d8): decade-spaced scales cross-faded by on-screen density, the base-uv derivative computed once and divided per scale so lines stay stable across the level boundaries. Written from the article.

### Color

- The cosine-gradient `CosinePalette` uses [Inigo Quilez's palette formula](https://iquilezles.org/articles/palettes/).
- The built-in `Palette` sets carry the eight ColorBrewer qualitative palettes ([colorbrewer2.org](https://colorbrewer2.org), Cynthia Brewer, Mark Harrower, and The Pennsylvania State University; Apache-2.0), as data.
- The `Colormap` ramps carry the canonical public colormap data: `viridis`/`magma`/`inferno`/`plasma`/`cividis` from [matplotlib](https://matplotlib.org) (CC0), `turbo` from Google (Apache-2.0), and `rocket`/`mako` from [seaborn](https://seaborn.pydata.org) (BSD-3).
- `Color(kelvin:)` maps a blackbody color temperature to sRGB with [Tanner Helland's approximation of the Planckian locus](https://tannerhelland.com/2012/09/18/convert-temperature-rgb-algorithm-code.html), written from the published formula.
- The perceptual color spaces (`OKLab`/`OKLCH`/`OKHSL`, and the gamut mapping behind `Color.mix`) implement [Björn Ottosson's Oklab](https://bottosson.github.io/posts/oklab/) and its [Okhsl picker space](https://bottosson.github.io/posts/colorpicker/), with out-of-gamut colors brought back by chroma reduction at constant lightness and hue following his [sRGB gamut-clipping method](https://bottosson.github.io/posts/gamutclipping/) (reference code public domain/MIT), written from the published math.
- The Adobe Swatch Exchange (`.ase`) reader is written from the community-documented description of the format at [selapa.net](http://www.selapa.net/swatches/colors/fileformats.php) and the [cyotek write-up](https://www.cyotek.com/blog/reading-adobe-swatch-exchange-ase-files-using-csharp). No code was taken from either. Its LAB swatches decode through CIELAB on the D50 white point using the standard inverse transfer and [Bruce Lindbloom's](http://www.brucelindbloom.com/index.html?Eqn_RGB_XYZ_Matrix.html) chromatically-adapted XYZ-to-sRGB matrix.
- Extracting a palette from an image clusters its pixels with Lloyd's algorithm ([Stuart Lloyd, "Least squares quantization in PCM"](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.1982.1056489)) seeded by k-means++ ([David Arthur and Sergei Vassilvitskii, "k-means++: The Advantages of Careful Seeding"](https://theory.stanford.edu/~sergei/papers/kMeansPP-soda.pdf)), run in Oklab so cluster distance tracks perceived difference. Written from the papers.
- Dithering an image down to a palette (`Image.dithered`) implements published methods, each written from the technique. The error-diffusion kernels are Floyd and Steinberg's ("An Adaptive Algorithm for Spatial Greyscale", *Proc. SID* 17/2, 1976), Jarvis, Judice and Ninke's ("A Survey of Techniques for the Display of Continuous Tone Pictures on Bilevel Displays", *Computer Graphics and Image Processing*, 1976), Peter Stucki's (*MECCA: A Multiple-Error Correcting Computation Algorithm*, IBM Research, 1981), Bill Atkinson's (the early Macintosh variant, which propagates only three quarters of the error), Daniel Burkes' (1988), and Frankie Sierra's three kernels (1989 to 1990); the coefficient tables were checked against [Tanner Helland's survey](https://tannerhelland.com/2012/12/28/dithering-eleven-algorithms-source-code.html) and Wikipedia's [error diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_diffusion) article. Ordered dithering uses Bryce Bayer's index matrix (*An Optimum Method for Two-Level Rendition of Continuous-Tone Pictures*, ICC 1973), built by the standard recurrence. The blue-noise threshold tile is generated by the void-and-cluster method ([Robert Ulichney, "The void-and-cluster method for dither array generation", *Proc. SPIE* 1913, 1993](https://cv.ulichney.com/papers/1993-void-cluster.pdf)), whose implementation shape was cross-read against [Alan Wolfe's write-up](https://blog.demofox.org/2019/06/25/generating-blue-noise-textures-with-void-and-cluster/). That the error must be accumulated in linear light rather than in sRGB, and that the quantizer should therefore choose in that same space, follows [Nayuki's "Gamma-aware image dithering"](https://www.nayuki.io/page/gamma-aware-image-dithering) and Surma's [Ditherpunk](https://surma.dev/things/ditherpunk/), which also notes the brightness bias of the naive Bayer threshold. The threshold maps choose each pixel's two-color mix by Joel Yliluoma's arbitrary-palette pair search ([Arbitrary-palette positional dithering algorithm](https://bisqwit.iki.fi/story/howto/dither/jy/)): every palette pair scored by how close its reachable mix looks, with his closed-form mixing ratio taken in linear light and his pair-noisiness penalty, here weighted by the mix's variance (his flat weighting suits the dense palettes he wrote for; on a sparse palette it trades the light end of a ramp for a solid band).
- The color-vision simulation (`ColorVision`, `Color.simulated(_:)`, `Filter.colorVision`) implements the physiologically-based model of Gustavo M. Machado, Manuel M. Oliveira and Leandro A. F. Fernandes, [*A Physiologically-based Model for Simulation of Color Vision Deficiency*](https://www.inf.ufrgs.br/~oliveira/pubs_files/CVD_Simulation/CVD_Simulation.html) (IEEE TVCG 15(6), 2009), which treats the deficiency as a shift in a cone's spectral absorption. The precomputed transformation matrices are the authors' published Table 1, applied in linear light as their derivation requires, with severities between the tabulated steps interpolated the way that page describes.
- `Palette.colorblindSafe` carries the eight-color qualitative set published by Masataka Okabe and Kei Ito for [color universal design](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/), chosen so its members stay apart under every kind of color vision.
- Print separations (`Image.separated(into:paper:)`, the `Ink` catalog, and `--export-separations`) are written from the standard prepress model for translucent spot inks: each ink acts as a transmittance filter (its color at full coverage on white stock), fractional coverage mixes the filter linearly in linear light (the single-ink collapse of the [Neugebauer halftone model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neugebauer_equations)), and layers screened at different angles multiply independently, which is also what makes the overprint preview honest. The per-color coverage search (nearest lattice seed refined by coordinate descent, judged in OKLab) follows the same linear-light/perceptual split as the dithering entry above. The per-layer halftone screens threshold against the covered-area function of a growing round dot (the classic clustered-dot spot-function screening of PostScript RIPs), rotated to the conventional prepress angles (45 degrees for the darkest ink, then 15, 75, and 0), with the standard minimum-dot highlight/shadow cutoff. The built-in ink catalog carries the community-measured screen values of the standard risograph ink line from [stencil.wiki's color list](https://www.stencil.wiki/colors), taken via Matt DesLauriers' [riso-colors](https://github.com/mattdesl/riso-colors) dataset (MIT). The workflow shape (one grayscale master per drum, black meaning full ink, a multiply-style overprint preview, per-ink screen angles) is the risograph community's standard practice, studied in [p5.riso](https://github.com/antiboredom/p5.riso) (Sam Lavigne and Tega Brain) and [Spectrolite](https://spectrolite.app) (Amelia Greenhall) as workflow references; no code was taken from either.

### 3D lighting & materials

- The 3D material finishes reimplement published shading techniques: the Blinn-Phong specular highlight (Jim Blinn, *Models of Light Reflection for Computer Synthesized Pictures*, SIGGRAPH 1977); the warm-to-cool **Gooch** model (Amy and Bruce Gooch, Peter Shirley, and Elaine Cohen, *A Non-Photorealistic Lighting Model for Automatic Technical Illustration*, SIGGRAPH 1998); and the Fresnel falloff behind the **rim** glow and the **iridescent** sheen (Christophe Schlick's approximation, *An Inexpensive BRDF Model for Physically-Based Rendering*, 1994), with the iridescent rainbow read from the same Inigo Quilez cosine palette above. The **sparkle** (metallic-flake) finish follows the sparkle term of the layered car-paint shader (Chris Oat, Natalya Tatarchuk, and John Isidoro, ["Layered Car Paint Shader"](https://www.chrisoat.com/papers/Oat-Tatarchuk-Isidoro-Layered_Car_Paint_Shader_Print.pdf), *ShaderX²*, 2003): a per-cell hash-jittered flake normal whose view alignment is sharply powered, realized procedurally (a world-space hash grid in place of the paper's microflake normal map) and written from the published equations. The cel (toon) shading is a common real-time idiom. The fake **subsurface** glow follows the cheap translucency approximation of [Colin Barré-Brisebois and Marc Bouchard, "Approximating Translucency for a Fast, Cheap and Convincing Subsurface Scattering Look"](https://colinbarrebrisebois.com/2011/03/07/gdc-2011-approximating-translucency-for-a-fast-cheap-and-convincing-subsurface-scattering-look/) (GDC 2011): a view-against-light transmittance lobe distorted toward the surface normal, plus an ambient floor, both weighted by a local-thickness stand-in (here a silhouette-thinness term, since the closed built-in forms carry no thickness map). Written from the talk's published equations.
- Triplanar projection (`Mesh.triplanarTextured`, texture for meshes with no uvs) implements the standard three-axis world projection blended by the surface normal, as described in [Ryan Geiss, "Generating Complex Procedural Terrains Using the GPU"](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems3/part-i-geometry/chapter-1-generating-complex-procedural-terrains-using-gpu) (*GPU Gems 3*, ch. 1, 2007), with the sharpened, renormalized blend weights of common real-time practice as surveyed in [Ben Golus's "Normal Mapping for a Triplanar Shader"](https://bgolus.medium.com/normal-mapping-for-a-triplanar-shader-10bf39dca05a) (2017), which is also the reference for projecting a tangent-space normal map per axis with a facing-sign flip; the per-plane normal combine (tangent offsets added, height components multiplied) is the "whiteout" blend of [Colin Barré-Brisebois and Stephen Hill, "Blending in Detail"](https://blog.selfshadow.com/publications/blending-in-detail/) (2012), applied per projection plane. The per-axis frames were re-derived in Ollin's own image conventions rather than transliterated, and the code is Ollin's own.
- Detail maps (`Mesh.detailMapped`, a finer texture pair tiled across the base one) blend the detail normal onto the base with **Reoriented Normal Mapping**, the quaternion-rotation blend of the same [Barré-Brisebois and Hill, "Blending in Detail"](https://blog.selfshadow.com/publications/blending-in-detail/) (2012), written from the paper's derivation; the gray-centered detail-color multiply is the long-standing detail-texturing idiom of the real-time engines. The code is Ollin's own.
- Projected decals (`decal(_:at:...)`, pictures stamped through an oriented box onto the surfaces inside it) implement the standard box-projection construction: transform the fragment's world position into the box's unit space, clip, and sample, with the surface-facing fade that keeps an edge-on receiver from smearing. The construction is described across the deferred/clustered decal literature, notably [Tiago Sousa and Jean Geffroy's "The Devil is in the Details: idTech 666"](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2016/Siggraph2016_idTech6.pdf) (SIGGRAPH 2016), read as an engineering reference for the forward-rendered arrangement; Ollin's small bounded list rides its own uniform beside the light-cookie machinery, and the code is Ollin's own.
- The physically-based **metallic-roughness** material (`Material.physicallyBased`) reimplements the standard Cook-Torrance microfacet model from the published math: the metallic-roughness parameterization (with α = roughness²) from [Brent Burley's *Physically-Based Shading at Disney*](https://blog.selfshadow.com/publications/s2012-shading-course/burley/s2012_pbs_disney_brdf_slides_v2.pdf) (SIGGRAPH 2012), the reference BRDF written out in the [glTF 2.0 specification's Appendix B](https://registry.khronos.org/glTF/specs/2.0/glTF-2.0.html), and the practical real-time forms (the fp16-safe GGX distribution and the height-correlated Smith visibility) from [Google's *Filament* documentation](https://google.github.io/filament/Filament.md.html). The Fresnel term is Schlick's approximation, cited above. Written independently from those references.
- The layered material lobes (`Material.clearcoat` / `Material.sheen`) reimplement the standard real-time constructions from the published references. The **clear coat** is the second Cook-Torrance lobe of [Google's *Filament* documentation](https://google.github.io/filament/Filament.md.html) and the [glTF `KHR_materials_clearcoat` extension](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/glTF/tree/main/extensions/2.0/Khronos/KHR_materials_clearcoat): GGX at the coat's own roughness with the [Kelemen-Szirmay-Kalos visibility](https://www.cg.tuwien.ac.at/research/publications/2001/Kelemen-2001-Coupled/) (Eurographics 2001), a fixed 0.04 Schlick Fresnel, the base attenuated by the coat's reflectance, and Filament's coat-interface F0 remapping. The **sheen** lobe is the "Charlie" inverted-alpha sine distribution from [Alejandro Conty Estevez and Christopher Kulla, *Production Friendly Microfacet Sheen BRDF*](https://fpsunflower.github.io/ckulla/data/s2017_pbs_imageworks_sheen.pdf) (SIGGRAPH 2017) with the visibility term of [David Neubelt and Matt Pettineo, *Crafting a Next-Gen Material Pipeline for The Order: 1886*](https://blog.selfshadow.com/publications/s2013-shading-course/rad/s2013_pbs_rad_notes.pdf) (SIGGRAPH 2013), layered by the directional-albedo energy scaling the [glTF `KHR_materials_sheen` extension](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/glTF/tree/main/extensions/2.0/Khronos/KHR_materials_sheen) and the [Enterprise PBR Shading Model](https://dassaultsystemes-technology.github.io/EnterprisePBRShadingModel/) specify (the albedo table baked at runtime by numerical integration, Filament's approach). Written independently from those references.
- The anisotropic specular finish (`Material.anisotropy`) reimplements the standard real-time anisotropic microfacet construction from the published references: the single-parameter split of one roughness into the two directional α values from [Christopher Kulla and Alejandro Conty, *Revisiting Physically Based Shading at Imageworks*](https://fpsunflower.github.io/ckulla/data/s2017_pbs_imageworks_slides_v2.pdf) (SIGGRAPH 2017), the anisotropic GGX distribution from Brent Burley's *Physically-Based Shading at Disney* (SIGGRAPH 2012, cited above), the height-correlated anisotropic Smith visibility from [Eric Heitz, *Understanding the Masking-Shadowing Function in Microfacet-Based BRDFs*](https://jcgt.org/published/0003/02/03/) (*JCGT* 3(2), 2014), the practical fp16-safe forms from [Google's *Filament* documentation](https://google.github.io/filament/Filament.md.html), and the bent-reflection-vector approximation for the environment gather from Stephen McAuley's *Rendering the World of Far Cry 4* (GDC 2015). Written independently from those references.
- Image-based lighting (`environment(_:)`) reimplements the **split-sum approximation** from [Brian Karis, "Real Shading in Unreal Engine 4"](https://cdn2.unrealengine.com/Resources/files/2013SiggraphPresentationsNotes-26915738.pdf) (SIGGRAPH 2013): a diffuse irradiance cubemap, a GGX-prefiltered specular mip-cube via importance sampling, and the environment-BRDF integration LUT, recombined at shade time. The GGX importance sampling uses the Hammersley sequence (van der Corput radical inverse). Written independently from the paper's listings.
- The live-feed environment (`Environment.feed(_:)`, a camera or video frame as the image-based-lighting source) follows the standard camera-feed lighting approach of augmented-reality practice: remap the captured frame onto the environment sphere, extrapolate the unseen part (here the frame's mirror image, pinched to its center column at the poles), and run the normal environment filtering over the result. The approach is what AR runtimes and web-AR engines do with camera imagery; cross-read against public write-ups such as [Zappar's *Approaching photorealistic AR on the web*](https://www.zappar.com/blog/approaching-photorealistic-ar-web/). The wrap mapping and the arrangement over the split-sum bake above are Ollin's own.
- The **soft shadows** (`shadowSoftness`, contact-hardening penumbrae for the directional and spot casters) use **Percentage-Closer Soft Shadows** (a blocker search over the shadow map, a penumbra width estimated from the receiver-to-blocker separation, then a variable-radius PCF kernel sized by it) from [Randima Fernando's *Percentage-Closer Soft Shadows*](https://developer.download.nvidia.com/shaderlibrary/docs/shadow_PCSS.pdf) (NVIDIA), with the perspective shadow map's depth linearized for the spot caster so the separation ratio stays meaningful; the technique was cross-read against openFrameworks' `shadow.glsl`. Written independently.
- The **contact shadows** (`contactShadows`, the short screen-space ray toward the casting light that seats resting objects) implement the screen-space shadow ray march as production renderers ship it, cross-read against three public references: [Unity's HDRP contact-shadow kernel](https://github.com/Unity-Technologies/Graphics/blob/master/Packages/com.unity.render-pipelines.high-definition/Runtime/Lighting/Shadow/ContactShadows.compute) (the camera-ward ray-start bias, the projected-segment march, and the slope-aware acceptance band derived from a view-axis ray's own depth span), [Panos Karabelas' screen-space shadows write-up](https://panoskarabelas.com/blog/posts/screen_space_shadows/) (MIT), and [Tomasz Stachowiak's depth-buffer raymarching gist](https://gist.github.com/h3r2tic/9c8356bdaefbe80b1a22ae0aaee192db) (Apache-2.0/MIT); the per-pixel start dither is Jorge Jimenez's interleaved gradient noise (*Next Generation Post Processing in Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare*, SIGGRAPH 2014 course), static here so exports reproduce. Written independently from the published methods.
- The ray-traced reflections (`rayTracedReflections()`, a physically-based metal reflecting the actual scene, off-screen geometry included, by tracing a reflection ray per reflective pixel against the scene's acceleration structure and shading the hit) follow the **hybrid rendering** approach (rasterize the primary surfaces, trace the reflection ray, shade the hit, fall back to the environment on a miss) from Apple's ["Explore hybrid rendering with Metal ray tracing"](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10150/) (WWDC21) and the ["Rendering reflections in real time using ray tracing"](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/metal_sample_code_library/rendering_reflections_in_real_time_using_ray_tracing) Metal sample, over Metal's inline `intersection_query` acceleration-structure tracing (the same native ray tracing behind the point-light shadows). Written from the technique, independently.
- The glossy reflection lobe (`glossyReflections()`, a rough surface's reflection traced as a spread lobe rather than one mirror ray) draws each ray from the **GGX distribution of visible normals** by the exact sampling routine of Eric Heitz, ["Sampling the GGX Distribution of Visible Normals"](https://jcgt.org/published/0007/04/01/) (*Journal of Computer Graphics Techniques* 7(4), 2018), and gathers the neighbors' rays back into one integral by the **ratio estimator** of the stochastic screen-space-reflection resolve (Tomasz Stachowiak, "Stochastic Screen-Space Reflections", *Advances in Real-Time Rendering*, SIGGRAPH 2015: reuse a neighbor's hit weighted by the local BRDF over the density that neighbor's ray was drawn with, and normalize by the summed weights). The speckle a stochastic reflection is otherwise known for is answered by **path-space roughness regularization** (widening every surface a ray reaches to the cone it arrived in, the real-time form of the regularization Anton Kaplanyan and Carsten Dachsbacher describe in ["Path Space Regularization for Holistic and Robust Light Transport"](https://cg.ivd.kit.edu/publications/p2013/PSR_Kaplanyan_2013/PSR_Kaplanyan_2013.pdf), *Eurographics* 2013). Written from the papers and the technique, independently; the talk's own lobe-truncation bias was measured here and rejected.
- The glass material (`Material.glass`, transmission and refraction) reimplements the ratified glTF material extensions [`KHR_materials_transmission`](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/glTF/blob/main/extensions/2.0/Khronos/KHR_materials_transmission/README.md) (the transmitted lobe replacing the diffuse one, tinted by the base color and blurred by the microfacet roughness), [`KHR_materials_volume`](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/glTF/blob/main/extensions/2.0/Khronos/KHR_materials_volume/README.md) (thickness turning a thin wall volumetric, with Beer-Lambert attenuation parameterized as the color white light becomes after a given distance), and [`KHR_materials_ior`](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/glTF/blob/main/extensions/2.0/Khronos/KHR_materials_ior/README.md) (normal-incidence Fresnel from the index of refraction). The environment-refraction base path (refract at entry, march the analytic interior span, approximate the exit interface with a curvature-blended normal, sample the prefiltered environment at a roughness-mapped mip, with the blur fading as the IOR nears 1) studies [Filament's refraction model](https://google.github.io/filament/Filament.html) (Google, Apache-2.0) as the engineering reference for those approximations; the ray-traced upgrade (tracing the refracted ray through the scene's acceleration structure, finding the solid's real exit interface at its back face) extends the same hybrid-rendering approach as the ray-traced reflections above. Written from the specifications and the technique, independently.
- Caustics (`caustics()`, the focused light glass and polished metal throw) reimplements **adaptive anisotropic photon scattering** from [Xueqing Yang and Yaobin Ouyang, *Real-Time Ray Traced Caustics*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4842-7185-8_30) (*Ray Tracing Gems II*, ch. 30, Apress 2021, open access): photon emission guided by a light-space ray-density map expanded into a cumulative quadtree task buffer, photon differentials tracked through every reflection and refraction to give each deposited photon an anisotropic elliptical footprint, additive screen-space splatting shaded with the receiver's G-buffer attributes, the negative-feedback density loop (projected footprint area against a target, plus back-projected temporal variance), and the perturbation-based dispersion approximation. The differentials follow [Homan Igehy, *Tracing Ray Differentials*](https://doi.org/10.1145/311535.311555) (SIGGRAPH 1999) and their photon form [Lars Schjøth, Jeppe Revall Frisvad, Kenny Erleben, and Jon Sporring, *Photon Differentials*](https://doi.org/10.1145/1321261.1321293) (GRAPHITE 2007); photon mapping itself is Henrik Wann Jensen's (*Realistic Image Synthesis Using Photon Mapping*, A K Peters, 2001). Written from the chapter's text, independently, over the same inline `intersection_query` tracing as the shadows, reflections, and global illumination; the chapter's separate water-surface technique is not implemented.
- Real-time global illumination (`globalIllumination()`) reimplements **dynamic diffuse global illumination** from [Zander Majercik, Jean-Philippe Guertin, Derek Nowrouzezahrai, and Morgan McGuire, *Dynamic Diffuse Global Illumination with Ray-Traced Irradiance Fields*](https://jcgt.org/published/0008/02/01/) (*Journal of Computer Graphics Techniques* 8(2), 2019): a uniform grid of irradiance probes, each storing octahedrally-mapped irradiance beside mean-distance/mean-squared-distance visibility moments, re-traced every frame with a rotated spherical-Fibonacci ray fan and sampled through the paper's visibility-aware weights (trilinear cage, soft backface, Chebyshev variance test). The production refinements come from the follow-up, [Majercik, Adam Marrs, Josef Spjut, and McGuire, *Scaling Probe-Based Real-Time Dynamic Global Illumination for Production*](https://jcgt.org/published/0010/02/01/) (*JCGT* 10(2), 2021): the unified self-shadow bias, the perception-based exponential irradiance encoding, the per-texel convergence heuristics, the zero-radiance/shortened-depth backface rule, and the probe-position relocation optimizer. The relocation's shipped form (statistics from a fixed, never-rotated ray subset; the backface-escape / frontface-backoff / drift-home branch set under an ellipsoid offset bound) and the irradiance blend's asymmetric per-texel response (a darkening lowers hysteresis, a large brightening is rate-limited) follow the production implementation of those papers, studied through NVIDIA's public RTXGI SDK documentation and shader sources; so does the vast-scene cascade tier's **infinite scrolling volume** (a camera-anchored probe window that moves in whole probe planes, wrapping each probe's storage index by a scroll phase so stationary probes keep their texels, and invalidating exactly the planes that scroll in: the SDK's `DDGIGetScrollingProbeIndex` / scrolled-plane-clear model, with Ollin folding the clear and the zeroed-hysteresis re-blend into one per-probe validity step). The nested doubling-spacing cascade arrangement follows the multi-volume practice that documentation describes; the ladder derivation from the camera's working distance, the slab-clamped axis counts, and the Metal implementation with its scale-free parameterization are Ollin's own. The octahedral mapping is from [Zina Cigolle, Sam Donow, Daniel Evangelakos, Michael Mara, McGuire, and Quirin Meyer, *A Survey of Efficient Representations for Independent Unit Vectors*](https://jcgt.org/published/0003/02/01/) (*JCGT* 3(2), 2014); the spherical Fibonacci ray fan follows [Ricardo Marques, Christian Bouville, Mickaël Ribardière, Luís Paulo Santos, and Kadi Bouatouch, *Spherical Fibonacci Point Sets for Illumination Integrals*](https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.12190) (*Computer Graphics Forum* 32(8), 2013); and the per-update random rotation is built by Ken Shoemake's uniform-quaternion construction (*Uniform Random Rotations*, Graphics Gems III, 1992). Written from the papers, independently, over the same inline `intersection_query` tracing as the shadows and reflections.
- The path-traced export (`--path-traced`) is a unidirectional Monte Carlo path tracer written from the published techniques: the light-transport formulation is [James T. Kajiya, *The Rendering Equation*](https://doi.org/10.1145/15922.15902) (SIGGRAPH 1986), with the practical estimator construction (next-event estimation against the light list, cosine-weighted diffuse sampling, and the environment's two-level luminance-CDF importance tables over the equirect's latitude-weighted rows) after the treatment in [*Physically Based Rendering*](https://pbr-book.org) (Matt Pharr, Wenzel Jakob, and Greg Humphreys). Specular directions sample the GGX distribution of visible normals per [Eric Heitz, *Sampling the GGX Distribution of Visible Normals*](https://jcgt.org/published/0007/04/01/) (*JCGT* 7(4), 2018); the environment and lobe strategies combine through the power heuristic of [Eric Veach and Leonidas J. Guibas, *Optimally Combining Sampling Techniques for Monte Carlo Rendering*](https://doi.org/10.1145/218380.218498) (SIGGRAPH 1995); lobe-side environment reads filter by the sampled probability's solid angle after [Mark Colbert and Jaroslav Křivánek, *GPU-Based Importance Sampling*](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems3/part-iii-rendering/chapter-20-gpu-based-importance-sampling) (*GPU Gems 3*, 2007); next-event evaluation at indirectly-seen polished surfaces widens its lobe per the path-space regularization of [Anton S. Kaplanyan and Carsten Dachsbacher, *Path Space Regularization for Holistic and Robust Light Transport*](https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.12026) (*Computer Graphics Forum* 32(2), 2013); unbiased termination is Russian roulette after [James Arvo and David Kirk, *Particle Transport and Image Synthesis*](https://doi.org/10.1145/97880.97886) (SIGGRAPH 1990); and the deterministic sample stream is the pcg4d counter-based hash from [Mark Jarzynski and Marc Olano, *Hash Functions for GPU Rendering*](https://jcgt.org/published/0009/03/02/) (*JCGT* 9(3), 2020). Transmission through the trace follows the rough-dielectric microfacet model of [Bruce Walter, Stephen R. Marschner, Hongsong Li, and Kenneth E. Torrance, *Microfacet Models for Refraction through Rough Surfaces*](https://www.graphics.cornell.edu/~bjw/microfacetbsdf.pdf) (EGSR 2007), sampled through the visible-normal distribution with stochastic Fresnel selection (Heitz, cited above), with the solid-interior absorption in the `attenuationColor` / `attenuationDistance` parameterization of the [glTF `KHR_materials_volume` extension](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/glTF/tree/main/extensions/2.0/Khronos/KHR_materials_volume); mesh-light next-event estimation (the power-proportional triangle CDF, uniform barycentric point sampling, and the area-to-solid-angle pdf) follows the *Physically Based Rendering* treatment cited above; and texture reads at hits pick their mip by the ray-cone method of [Tomas Akenine-Möller, Jim Nilsson, Magnus Andersson, Colin Barré-Brisebois, Robert Toth, and Tero Karras, *Texture Level of Detail Strategies for Real-Time Ray Tracing*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4842-4427-2_20) (*Ray Tracing Gems*, 2019). The grain filter that runs over the finished render is the edge-avoiding a-trous wavelet of [Holger Dammertz, Daniel Sewtz, Johannes Hanika, and Hendrik P. A. Lensch, *Edge-Avoiding À-Trous Wavelet Transform for fast Global Illumination Filtering*](https://jo.dreggn.org/home/2010_atrous.pdf) (HPG 2010), with the albedo demodulation and the variance-driven luminance weight (including the small blur of the variance itself before it is used) from [Christoph Schied, Anton Kaplanyan, Chris Wyman, Anjul Patney, Chakravarty R. Alla Chaitanya, John Burgess, Shiqiu Liu, Carsten Dachsbacher, Aaron Lefohn, and Marco Salvi, *Spatiotemporal Variance-Guided Filtering: Real-Time Reconstruction for Path-Traced Global Illumination*](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2017-07_spatiotemporal-variance-guided-filtering-real-time-reconstruction-path-traced) (HPG 2017); that method's temporal half is deliberately left out, since an offline still has no previous frame, so the variance is measured across each pixel's own samples instead. Written from the papers, independently, over the same inline `intersection_query` tracing as the shadows, reflections, global illumination, and caustics.
- The area lights (`rectLight` / `diskLight` / `tubeLight`) reimplement **linearly transformed cosines**: the rect panel's horizon-clipped polygon integral from [Eric Heitz, Jonathan Dupuy, Stephen Hill, and David Neubelt, *Real-Time Polygonal-Light Shading with Linearly Transformed Cosines*](https://eheitzresearch.wordpress.com/415-2/) (SIGGRAPH 2016); the disk's transformed-ellipse evaluation (eigendecomposition, a cubic solve for the horizon, and the tabulated horizon-clipped sphere) from [Heitz and Dupuy's sphere- and disk-light extension](https://eheitzresearch.wordpress.com/757-2/); and the tube's analytic line integral from [Eric Heitz and Stephen Hill, *Linear-Light Shading with Linearly Transformed Cosines*](https://eheitzresearch.wordpress.com/758-2/). The disk path's cubic roots use the numerically robust split form from [Christoph Peters, "How to solve a cubic equation, revisited"](http://momentsingraphics.de/?p=105). The shaders are written from the papers and the technique's published evaluation; the *fitted tables* are bundled from the authors' BSD-licensed reference release (see *Bundled third-party code* below).
- Spatial video (`--export-spatial`) shoots its stereo pairs on the **parallel-axis off-axis rig** that is standard stereographic practice: two cameras a fixed distance apart looking parallel, with each frustum sheared so the two views coincide at the convergence distance, rather than toed in at the subject, which tilts the frames against each other and leaves vertical parallax at the corners (Lenny Lipton, *Foundations of the Stereoscopic Cinema*, Van Nostrand Reinhold, 1982, the standard reference for both the rig and the reason). The derived eye spacing follows the published comfort guidance rather than a chosen constant: the [International Stereoscopic Union's golden rules](https://isu3d.org/goldenrules/) put the deviation across a picture at no more than about a thirtieth of its width and hold that no viewer should ever be made to diverge their eyes, and cinema practice tightens the far-field figure to around one percent of screen width, which is the criterion Ollin's default is written to. The file format is Apple's documented [MV-HEVC spatial video](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfoundation/converting-side-by-side-3d-video-to-multiview-hevc-and-spatial-video), written against the published AVFoundation and VideoToolbox surfaces. The geometry, the derivation, and the writer are Ollin's own.
- Light shaping reads **IES photometric files** per the published [IESNA LM-63 / ANSI/IES LM-63 standard](https://blog.ansi.org/ansi/standard-file-photometric-data-ies-lm-63-19/) (the Type C angle conventions, the lateral-symmetry rules keyed to the last horizontal angle, and TILT handling), with [Sébastien Lagarde's format walkthrough](https://seblagarde.wordpress.com/2014/11/05/ies-light-format-specification-and-reader/) and Ian Ashdown's public parser notes studied for the spec's real-world ambiguities (the parser is Ollin's own). Baking a normalized profile into a small angular texture that scales a light's intensity is the standard real-time treatment described in [*Moving Frostbite to PBR*](https://seblagarde.wordpress.com/2015/07/14/siggraph-2014-moving-frostbite-to-physically-based-rendering/) (Lagarde and de Rousiers, SIGGRAPH 2014 course) and [Unreal Engine's IES light profiles](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/using-ies-light-profiles-in-unreal-engine); the bundled example `.ies` files are authored for Ollin, not manufacturer data. **Light cookies** (`LightCookie` on a spot) are classic projective texture mapping, the gobo/light-function idea every real-time engine ships; the cone-footprint mapping and the code are Ollin's own.
- Real subsurface scattering (`Material.scattering` / `.skin` / `.marble`) reimplements **separable subsurface scattering** from [Jorge Jimenez, Károly Zsolnai, Adrián Jarabo, Christian Freude, Thomas Auzinger, Xian-Chun Wu, Javier von der Pahlen, Michael Wimmer, and Diego Gutierrez, *Separable Subsurface Scattering*](http://www.iryoku.com/separable-sss/) (*Computer Graphics Forum* 34(6), 2015): a screen-space diffusion as two 1D convolutions over the linear pre-tonemap frame, the taps importance-distributed and weighted by a diffusion profile, with a depth-aware step scale and a depth-gap guard at silhouettes. The profile is the sum-of-Gaussians fit of measured skin reflectance from [Eugene d'Eon and David Luebke, *Advanced Techniques for Realistic Real-Time Skin Rendering*](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems3/part-iii-rendering/chapter-14-advanced-techniques-realistic-real-time-skin-rendering) (*GPU Gems 3*, ch. 14, 2007), one channel's curve stretched per channel by the material's falloff color as the separable-SSS work does. The authors' BSD-licensed reference implementation was studied for the kernel construction and integration details; the code (the CPU kernel build, the mask pass, and the Metal blur) is Ollin's own, and the depth-gap guard is re-derived scale-invariant (measured against the scattering radius rather than the reference's screen-space constant, which saturates when the radius is a visible fraction of the object). The transmittance term (light through thin backlit geometry, under a shadow-casting light) reimplements the shadow-map translucency of [Jorge Jimenez, David Whelan, Veronica Sundstedt, and Diego Gutierrez, *Real-Time Realistic Skin Translucency*](https://www.iryoku.com/translucency/) (*IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications* 30(4), 2010): thickness estimated as the gap between the receiver and the caster's stored depth (the receiver shrunk along its normal, their silhouette fix), a transmittance profile derived by integrating the diffusion profile over a slab, and the reversed-normal irradiance with the paper's 0.3 wrap; here the profile is the closed-form slab integral of the same Gaussian sum the diffusion kernel uses, so the two halves share one material description. On the directional/spot (2D-map) path the thickness read gathers over a lateral neighborhood of entry points sized by the scattering radius, the same diffusion profile evaluated over each tap's through-body path, which is the light-space gathering idea of [Carsten Dachsbacher and Marc Stamminger, *Translucent Shadow Maps*](https://doi.org/10.2312/EGWR/EGWR03/197-201) (*Eurographics Workshop on Rendering*, 2003), expressed over the Gaussian sum above and normalized so a constant-thickness slab reduces exactly to the slab form. Written from the papers and the technique.
- The volumetric cloudscape over the procedural sky (`Environment.clouds(_:)`) reimplements the published real-time cloud rendering technique: Andrew Schneider and Nathan Vos, [*The Real-Time Volumetric Cloudscapes of Horizon Zero Dawn*](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/read/the-real-time-volumetric-cloudscapes-of-horizon-zero-dawn) (SIGGRAPH 2015 Advances in Real-Time Rendering course) and Schneider's follow-up [*Nubis: Authoring Real-Time Volumetric Cloudscapes with the Decima Engine*](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/read/nubis-authoring-real-time-volumetric-cloudscapes-with-the-decima-engine) (SIGGRAPH 2017): the tiling Perlin-Worley base and Worley erosion volumes, the weather-map coverage remap, the height-shaping pair, and the Beer-law march with its silver-lining phase and edge-darkening terms. The parameterization was cross-read against Fredrik Häggström, [*Real-time rendering of volumetric clouds*](http://umu.diva-portal.org/smash/record.jsf?pid=diva2:1223894) (Umeå University, 2018), whose write-up states the recipe's equations explicitly. Written independently from the published methods; the weather map is generated procedurally rather than authored.
- The atmosphere (`fog` / `aerialPerspective` / `volumetricLight`) reimplements published techniques. The distance and height fog use [Inigo Quilez's closed-form fog integrals](https://iquilezles.org/articles/fog/) (the exponential-height density whose line integral has an exact solution, applied per fragment). The aerial perspective follows [Naty Hoffman and Arcot J. Preetham, *Rendering Outdoor Light Scattering in Real Time*](https://renderwonk.com/publications/gdc-2002/hoffman.pdf) (GDC 2002): the single-scattering solution's two factors, a wavelength-dependent extinction (Rayleigh RGB coefficients in their measured sea-level ratios plus a gray aerosol term) and a closed-form sun in-scatter mixing the Rayleigh phase with Henyey-Greenstein, applied over the same closed-form optical depth as the fog. The volumetric light march follows [Balázs Tóth and Tamás Umenhoffer, *Real-time Volumetric Lighting in Participating Media*](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.2312/egs.20091048.057-060) (Eurographics 2009 Short Papers): single scattering accumulated along the view ray with a shadow-map visibility test per step, here bounded per spot light to the ray's analytic cone crossing so thin beams stay resolvable. The angular distribution is the [Henyey-Greenstein phase function](https://www.astro.princeton.edu/~draine/papers/HG41.pdf) (1941), and the march's deterministic per-pixel jitter is Jorge Jimenez's interleaved gradient noise (*Next Generation Post Processing in Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare*, SIGGRAPH 2014 course). Written independently from the published methods.

### Effects, compute & simulation

- The image-`Filter` catalog (color, tone, blur, stylize, retro, and distortion passes) reimplements well-known image-processing techniques, each written from the published method: the **oil-paint** abstraction is the Kuwahara region filter (Kyprianidis et al., ["Anisotropic Kuwahara Filtering on the GPU", *GPU Pro*](https://www.kyprianidis.com/)); the **3×3 median** uses Morgan McGuire's [min/max sorting-network median](https://casual-effects.com/research/McGuire2008Median/); **vibrance** follows CeeJayDK's [SweetFX](https://github.com/CeeJayDK/SweetFX) formulation; **bilateral** smoothing, **emboss**, **Sobel** edges/**normalMap**, **CMYK halftone**, **solarize**, **levels**, **scanlines**/**glitch**/**CRT**, and the **kaleidoscope**/**swirl**/**bulge**/**wave**/**ripple**/**polar**/**perturb** uv warps are standard real-time idioms (the radial-lens warps after Inigo Quilez and the [LYGIA](https://lygia.xyz) `distort` catalog, read for technique). The ordered **dither** (both the channel-quantizing and two-tone forms) uses Bryce Bayer's index matrix (*An Optimum Method for Two-Level Rendition of Continuous-Tone Pictures*, ICC 1973); **relight** combines the Sobel normal-from-height above with the Blinn-Phong specular model (Jim Blinn, SIGGRAPH 1977, credited in full under the 3D materials), each finish a tuned diffuse/specular/rim recipe over that shared surface. The branchless RGB↔HSV behind `colorama` is Sam Hocevar's. The full menu was cross-read against LYGIA, [ofxFX](https://github.com/patriciogonzalezvivo/ofxFX), OPENRNDR's [orx-fx](https://github.com/openrndr/orx), [AsyncGraphics](https://github.com/heestand-xyz/AsyncGraphics) (which set the effect-object/compositing shape), and [fluid](https://github.com/enonforetsam/fluid) (MIT; the relight-with-finishes, cursor-centered-warp, and two-tone-dither ideas) for which effects exist and how they're approached, then written independently.
- The **Hopf fibration** (`hopfFibers`) is the map Heinz Hopf described in ["Über die Abbildungen der dreidimensionalen Sphäre auf die Kugelfläche"](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01457962) (Mathematische Annalen, 1931): every point of a two-sphere lifting to a great circle of the three-sphere, no two of which meet and every two of which are linked exactly once. The explicit parameterization of each circle, and the stereographic projection that brings it into space, follow the presentation in David W. Lyons, ["An Elementary Introduction to the Hopf Fibration"](https://doi.org/10.2307/3219300) (Mathematics Magazine, 2003). Written from those, independently.
- **`RadialBasis`** puts a smooth field through scattered values by the method Rolland L. Hardy introduced as multiquadric interpolation, ["Multiquadric equations of topography and other irregular surfaces"](https://doi.org/10.1029/JB076i008p01905) (Journal of Geophysical Research, 1971). Its default kernel is the thin-plate spline of Jean Duchon, ["Splines minimizing rotation-invariant semi-norms in Sobolev spaces"](https://doi.org/10.1007/BFb0086566) (1977), the surface through the data with the least bending energy; the degree-one polynomial fitted alongside the bumps is the standard augmentation that makes a conditionally positive definite kernel uniquely solvable. **`Fit.minimize`** walks its knobs by the adaptive per-parameter step of Diederik P. Kingma and Jimmy Ba, ["Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6980) (ICLR 2015), over slopes measured by central differences rather than derived, since the cost is a closure the sketch writes. Written from the papers, independently.
- The **local averages** (`Filter.boxBlur`, `Filter.adaptiveThreshold`) stand on the **summed-area table** of Franklin C. Crow, ["Summed-Area Tables for Texture Mapping"](https://doi.org/10.1145/800031.808600) (SIGGRAPH 1984): every texel holding the sum of everything above and to the left of it, so the sum over any rectangle is four lookups. It is built by the recursive-doubling scan of Justin Hensley, Thorsten Scheuermann, Greg Coombe, Montek Singh and Anselmo Lastra, ["Fast Summed-Area Table Generation and its Applications"](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2005.00880.x) (Eurographics 2005), whose recommendation to bias each element by -0.5 before accumulating is followed here for the precision it buys back. The local cut is the method of Derek Bradley and Gerhard Roth, ["Adaptive Thresholding Using the Integral Image"](https://doi.org/10.1080/2151237X.2007.10129236) (Journal of Graphics Tools, 2007): each pixel compared against the mean of its own neighborhood, a fraction below which it goes dark. Ollin makes that comparison in linear light rather than on gamma-encoded bytes, since illumination multiplies what a surface returns and only a linear space makes that a plain scale. Written from the papers, independently.
- The **measured distance field** (`Filter.distanceField`) is the jump-flooding algorithm of Guodong Rong and Tiow-Seng Tan, ["Jump Flooding in GPU with Applications to Voronoi Diagram and Distance Transform"](https://doi.org/10.1145/1111411.1111431) (I3D 2006): a seed's position passed outward over a ladder of halving step sizes, so the whole layer is flooded in a number of passes that grows with the logarithm of its size. The extra opening pass with a step of one is their [1+JFA variant](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/jfa-variants/jfa-variants.pdf) ("Variants of Jump Flooding Algorithm for Computing Discrete Voronoi Diagrams", ISVD 2007), which costs the same as the JFA+1 variant and makes far fewer errors. Placing each seed at the sub-pixel crossing found between two pixels, rather than at the pixel that holds it, is the standard practice for measuring an antialiased edge. Written from the papers, independently.
- The **iridescence** filter colors its rainbow sheen by thin-film interference (per-channel reflectance at one representative wavelength per primary, the standard optics of a [soap film / oil slick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin-film_interference), cross-read against Alan Zucconi's [thin-film car-paint series](https://www.alanzucconi.com/2017/10/27/carpaint-shader-thin-film-interference/), read for technique) over a film-thickness field built with [Inigo Quilez's domain warping](https://iquilezles.org/articles/warp/) (fbm fed its own noise). The **glitter** filter's hash-cell sparkle construction (a jittered fleck per cell with a random twinkle phase, sharp `pow(sin)` flicker, and the `|x·y|` hyperbola cross flare scanned over the 3×3 neighbor cells) is the starfield/sparkle idiom canonicalized by Martijn Steinrucken's ["Shader Coding: Making a starfield"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rvDo9LvfoVE) (The Art of Code; his tutorial shader is CC BY-NC-SA, read for the technique only), with the sub-pixel fade-by-footprint after the glint LOD idea in Zirr & Kaplanyan's ["Real-time Rendering of Procedural Multiscale Materials"](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2016-02_Real-time-Rendering-of/ZirrKaplanyan_MultiscaleI3D2016.pdf) (I3D 2016). Both written from the techniques, independently.
- The depth-of-field effect filter (`.defocus`, blurring a layer by a depth map) is a circle-of-confusion bokeh gather with near/far field separation, combining two techniques: the **near/far split** (foreground accumulated with its own coverage and composited over the background, so a blurry foreground hides an in-focus subject behind it) from Catlike Coding's [Depth of Field](https://catlikecoding.com/unity/tutorials/advanced-rendering/depth-of-field/) tutorial, and the per-field **running-average golden-angle gather** (every tap counts, so overlapping bokeh blends without noise) from Dennis Gustafsson's [Bokeh depth of field in a single pass](http://blog.tuxedolabs.com/2018/05/04/bokeh-depth-of-field-in-single-pass.html) (Tuxedo Labs), studied through its expression in [LYGIA](https://lygia.xyz)'s `sample/dof` (Prosperity-licensed, read for the technique, reimplemented). James George's openFrameworks `ofxDOF` is further prior art (no license, concept only). Written independently.
- The depth-of-field accumulation (sandpainting) rendering (bokeh that *emerges* from scattering many faint additive samples by their distance from a focal plane, with chromatic fringes from shifting the color channels apart) follows Anders Hoff's ([inconvergent](https://inconvergent.net/2019/depth-of-field/)) depth-of-field and color-shift technique, written from the essays. [Blurry](https://github.com/Domenicobrz/Blurry) (Domenicobrz, MIT) is the reference implementation, read for approach and written independently.
- The ambient-occlusion combine (`.ambientOcclusion`, darkening a layer's crevices by a depth map) reconstructs view-space position and normal from the depth (the normal via the best-of-paired-neighbors method from [Wicked Engine](https://wickedengine.net/2019/09/improved-normal-reconstruction-from-depth/)'s "improved normal reconstruction from depth"), then estimates occlusion with a view-space hemisphere kernel (the canonical SSAO recipe: Crytek's original; [john-chapman](https://john-chapman-graphics.blogspot.com/2013/01/ssao-tutorial.html) / [LearnOpenGL](https://learnopengl.com/Advanced-Lighting/SSAO), with the standard depth range-check), using a **dense low-discrepancy (Fibonacci) kernel** so it needs no per-pixel rotation and stays temporally stable, finished with a depth-aware blur. Written from the techniques, independently.
- The screen-space-reflections combine (`.screenSpaceReflections`, reflecting the scene off its own surfaces by a depth map) reflects each pixel's eye ray about its surface normal and traces the reflection through the depth buffer as a screen-space DDA (adaptive stride covering the whole ray in a fixed step budget, perspective-correct depth, a binary-refined hit), accepting a hit where the ray's depth interval crosses a surface and the surface faces the ray, then composites the scene color back with Fresnel, edge, and distance falloff. The screen-space ray trace follows McGuire & Mara's ["Efficient GPU Screen-Space Ray Tracing"](https://jcgt.org/published/0003/04/04/) (the canonical DDA), [Sugu Lee's SSR write-up](https://sugulee.wordpress.com/2021/01/16/performance-optimizations-for-screen-space-reflections-technique-part-1-linear-tracing-method/), the [3D Game Shaders For Beginners](https://lettier.github.io/3d-game-shaders-for-beginners/screen-space-reflection.html) tutorial, and Patricio Gonzalez Vivo's [LYGIA](https://lygia.xyz) `lighting/ssr` (read for the line-distance hit test and back-face rejection). Written from the techniques, independently.
- The SSR temporal resolve (reprojecting the previous frame's reflection by the camera's motion and accumulating it across frames, with a neighborhood variance clamp on the history to reject ghosting) follows Brian Karis's "High-Quality Temporal Supersampling" (SIGGRAPH 2014) and the temporal reprojection of Lasse Jon Fuglsang Pedersen / Playdead ("Temporal Reprojection Anti-Aliasing in INSIDE", GDC 2016). Written from the technique, independently.
- The SSR spatial resolve (a depth-aware despeckle and a variance-aware gloss blur over the reflection) follows the stochastic screen-space reflection denoise of Frostbite (Tomasz Stachowiak, "Stochastic Screen-Space Reflections", SIGGRAPH 2015) and AMD's FidelityFX SSSR. Written from the technique, independently.
- Temporal anti-aliasing (`temporalAntialiasing()`: sub-pixel projection jitter accumulated across frames with history reprojection and neighborhood rectification) is written from the published treatments: Brian Karis's "High-Quality Temporal Supersampling" (SIGGRAPH 2014; the Halton (2,3) jitter, the remove-the-jitter velocity rule, the YCoCg neighborhood box, the luminance-compressed blend, and the Blackman-Harris-fit current-frame reconstruction filter), Marco Salvi's moment-based variance clipping ("An Excursion in Temporal Supersampling", GDC 2016), Lasse Jon Fuglsang Pedersen / Playdead's closest-depth velocity dilation ("Temporal Reprojection Anti-Aliasing in INSIDE", GDC 2016), and Lei Yang, Shiqiu Liu & Marco Salvi's survey ("A Survey of Temporal Antialiasing Techniques", *Computer Graphics Forum* 39(2), Eurographics 2020 STAR; the Catmull-Rom history resampling and the motion-adaptive clip width follow its recommendations and the production practice it catalogs). The camera-only reprojection first stage follows the lineage the survey records (Tiago Sousa's CryENGINE 3 treatment, SIGGRAPH 2011). The mover-velocity buffer (`withMotion`: a per-object velocity pass whose stored convention, previous minus current in render-resolution pixels with the jitter excluded, matches the motion-texture contract of Apple's MetalFX temporal scaler as documented in ["Boost performance with MetalFX Upscaling"](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10103/), WWDC22) follows the same Karis velocity rules and Pedersen closest-depth dilation; the sparse movers-plus-camera-fallback population pattern was cross-read against production practice (Unreal's movable-only velocity buffer, Unity's per-renderer motion-vector modes, Playdead's camera-baseline pass) as engineering references. Written from the techniques, independently; the deterministic export path (N jittered renders averaged within one frame) and the call-site mover identity are Ollin's own arrangements.
- Lens flare (`lensFlare()`: the ghosts a lens throws when light reflects off two of its own interfaces and reaches the sensor anyway) is written from the matrix formulation of Sungkil Lee & Elmar Eisemann, ["Practical Real-Time Lens-Flare Rendering"](https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.12145) (*Computer Graphics Forum* 32(4), EGSR 2013): the first-order (paraxial) ray transfer matrices for travel, refraction at a spherical interface, and reflection, composed along each two-reflection path so that a ghost maps the entrance pupil to the sensor and to the iris plane by a linear map, with both bounces kept on one side of the aperture. The `Lens.heliar` prescription is the Heliar Tronnier table that paper tabulates from the patent literature (USP 2645156, long expired); a lens prescription is factual data, not code. That work builds on Matthias Hullin, Elmar Eisemann, Hans-Peter Seidel & Sungkil Lee, ["Physically-Based Real-Time Lens Flare Rendering"](https://doi.org/10.1145/2010324.1965003) (*SIGGRAPH* 2011), whose supplemental material is also the source for the anti-reflective coating reflectance: a quarter-wave layer at the geometric mean index, with the two faces' Fresnel amplitudes interfered per polarization over the path-difference phase, which is what colors a ghost. (Their patent application on the ray-traced method was abandoned in the US and withdrawn in Europe; nothing in that family is in force.) Both are written from the papers, independently. The star on the source is the same pair of papers' other half: far-field (Fraunhofer) diffraction at the iris, where the pattern reaching the sensor is the power spectrum of the opening's own image, sampled per wavelength at a scale going as 1/lambda so the arms fan into color. Each wavelength's color comes from the analytic fit to the CIE 1931 color matching functions of Chris Wyman, Peter-Pike Sloan & Peter Shirley, ["Simple Analytic Approximations to the CIE XYZ Color Matching Functions"](https://jcgt.org/published/0002/02/01/) (*JCGT* 2(2), 2013), converted to linear sRGB by the standard matrix. The per-surface coating wavelengths (`Lens.multicoated`), the blend from the coated formula to bare Fresnel as an angle approaches total internal reflection, the treatment of a cemented junction as uncoated, the source-visibility term read from the scene's own depth, the backwards per-pixel evaluation in place of rasterized flare quads, and the mean-normalized bake with its edge window are Ollin's own.
- The shape an out-of-focus highlight takes in the screen-space depth of field (`.defocus(blades:irisAngle:catsEye:)`) follows two published ideas, each written from the technique. That a post-process gathering depth-of-field shader can carry a polygonal rather than a round opening, and that the opening is what a highlight is a picture of, is the subject of Lawrence McIntosh, Bernhard E. Riecke & Steve DiPaola, ["Efficiently Simulating the Bokeh of Polygonal Apertures in a Post-Process Depth of Field Shader"](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2012.02097.x) (*Computer Graphics Forum* 31(6), 2012). Their own method is a separable low-rank filter, which Ollin does not use: the shipped gather already visits taps in an expanding spiral, so the opening enters as the reach its own boundary allows in each direction, and a polygon is taken at the area of the round opening it replaces so a blade count changes the shape of a highlight without changing how large it reads. The clipping toward the corners is optical vignetting, whose geometry (the pupil an off-axis image point actually sees is what is left of the aperture after the barrel's own openings are projected onto it) is the effect Craig Kolb, Don Mitchell & Pat Hanrahan's ["A Realistic Camera Model for Computer Graphics"](https://doi.org/10.1145/218380.218463) (*SIGGRAPH* 1995) reproduces by tracing rays through a whole lens system. Ollin reproduces the shape rather than the system: the opening is intersected with two discs pushed apart along the line to the middle of the frame, which is the symmetric lemon a real barrel leaves. Reading the blade count off the camera the depth layer was drawn with, so the live blur, the flare ghosts, and the path-traced export cannot disagree about one lens, is Ollin's own.
- Motion blur (`motionBlur()`: a full-screen velocity field reduced to a per-tile dominant velocity, then a per-pixel gather along it with continuous depth-and-blurriness classification) reimplements the plausible-motion-blur reconstruction filter of Morgan McGuire, Padraic Hennessy, Michael Bukowski & Brian Osman, ["A Reconstruction Filter for Plausible Motion Blur"](https://casual-effects.com/research/McGuire2012Blur/McGuire12Blur.pdf) (*I3D* 2012): the half-spread velocity clamped to [0.5 px, k], the TileMax/NeighborMax dominant-velocity pyramid, and the three-case cone/cylinder/soft-depth-compare gather with a jittered tap comb. The velocity field itself is the mover-velocity buffer above with a depth-reprojected camera baseline filled in per pixel (the same fill the MetalFX motion contract expects full-screen). Written from the paper, independently; the resolution-relative tile size, the scene-scaled soft-depth extent, and the deterministic export arrangement (frame k reading frame k-1's camera and movers through the headless drive) are Ollin's own.
- Temporal upscaling (`temporalUpscaling()`) drives **Apple's MetalFX temporal scaler**, a platform framework used as-is rather than a technique reimplemented: Ollin supplies the jittered reduced-resolution render, the depth resolve, and its own full-screen raw velocity fill (the mover-velocity buffer with the camera baseline filled in), following the contracts documented in ["Boost performance with MetalFX Upscaling"](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10103/) (WWDC22) and the framework headers. The deterministic export arrangement (an upscaling sketch exporting as the full-resolution temporal-AA supersample) is Ollin's own.
- The simulation fields (`SimField`/`Sim`, a layer that evolves on the GPU each frame) reimplement classic cellular models, written from the technique: **reaction-diffusion** is the Gray-Scott model (John E. Pearson, ["Complex Patterns in a Simple System"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.261.5118.189), *Science* 1993), with the GPU discretization and the dividing-cell feed/kill defaults following Karl Sims' [reaction-diffusion tutorial](https://www.karlsims.com/rd.html); **Game of Life** is John Conway's cellular automaton (B3/S23); **Lenia** is Bert Wang-Chak Chan's continuous cellular automaton (["Lenia - Biology of Artificial Life"](https://doi.org/10.25088/ComplexSystems.28.3.251), *Complex Systems* 28.3, 2019), implemented from the paper's definitions (the exponential kernel core copied into concentric rings, the sum-normalized convolution, the exponential growth mapping, and the clipped dt integration), with the Orbium parameters as defaults. All run as fragment passes over the persistent ping-pong layer, written independently.
- The **multi-scale Turing patterns** (`.multiScaleTuring`) reimplement Jonathan McCabe's elaboration of Turing morphogenesis, written from his paper ["Cyclic Symmetric Multi-Scale Turing Patterns"](http://www.jonathanmccabe.com/Cyclic_Symmetric_Multi-Scale_Turing_Patterns.pdf) (*Proceedings of Bridges 2010: Mathematics, Music, Art, Architecture, Culture*), itself derived from Alan Turing's ["The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis"](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1952.0012) (*Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 237, 1952): a single substance whose activator and inhibitor are averages over a small and a large area, several such scales run at once, and at each pixel and step the scale with the least variation firing, with cyclic symmetry imposed by averaging each point's averages with its counterparts around the circle. Jason Rampe's ["Multi-Scale Turing Patterns"](https://softologyblog.wordpress.com/2011/07/05/multi-scale-turing-patterns/) (Softology, 2011) was read for the per-scale variation radius the paper leaves implicit. The GPU realization is our own: a Gaussian (Burt-Adelson) blur pyramid serving every radius, gathered as a disc two rungs finer than the radius so the pattern does not lock to the pyramid's lattice, and a min/max reduction chain driving the per-step renormalization. Ricky Reusser's [FFT-based WebGPU implementation](https://rreusser.github.io/notebooks/multiscale-turing-patterns/) and [janivanecky's](https://github.com/janivanecky/multiscale-turing-patterns) were noted as prior GPU treatments, not read for code.
- The **watercolor wash** (`.watercolor` / `WatercolorField` / `WatercolorPigment`) reimplements the classic three-layer watercolor simulation of Cassidy J. Curtis, Sean E. Anderson, Joshua E. Seims, Kurt W. Fleischer & David H. Salesin, ["Computer-Generated Watercolor"](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/watercolor/) (*SIGGRAPH* 1997), written from the paper: the shallow-water layer on a staggered grid (whose equations follow the liquid-animation work the paper itself cites, "Realistic Animation of Liquids", *Graphical Models and Image Processing* 58, 1996, including the iterative divergence relaxation), the pigment-deposition layer with per-pigment density, staining, and granulation, the capillary layer whose saturation diffusion and wet-area-mask expansion produce backruns, the blurred-mask edge darkening, the dry-brush height threshold, and the Kubelka-Munk optical layer model (after Paul Kubelka's layer equations; the paper's forms) with the paper's inversion that derives absorption and scattering from a pigment's color over white and over black. The measured coefficients behind the twelve pigment presets are the paper's published Figure 5 table. Deliberate implementation decisions of ours, documented in `ARCHITECTURE.md`: the viscosity term follows the paper's continuous equations (+μ∇²u; the printed pseudocode's sign reads inverted, and anti-diffusion is unstable), the relaxation is implemented in the divergence-reducing direction, the adaptive time step becomes fixed CFL substeps with clamped velocities (the ripples precedent), the unspecified capillary constants are calibrated and exposed as knobs, and the paper's authored damp-region initial conditions surface as `blot()`. The GPU realization (gather-form fragments over persistent ping-pong pairs, the dried-glaze reflectance/transmittance stack) is our own.
- The **Abelian sandpile** (`.sandpile`) reimplements the Bak-Tang-Wiesenfeld model, written from the papers: Per Bak, Chao Tang & Kurt Wiesenfeld, ["Self-organized criticality: An explanation of the 1/f noise"](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.59.381) (*Physical Review Letters* 59, 1987) defines the threshold-4 toppling automaton, and Deepak Dhar's ["Self-organized critical state of sandpile automaton models"](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.64.1613) (*Physical Review Letters* 64, 1990) proves the abelian property (topplings commute) that makes the GPU's parallel update, every unstable cell toppling as many times as it can per pass, settle into the same configuration as any sequential order. The GPU realization (a gather fragment over the persistent ping-pong layer, grain counts stored in quarters for exact half-float arithmetic, an open dissipative boundary) is our own.
- The **state automata** sims reimplement four classic published rules, each written from the paper or its canonical popularization: the **cyclic cellular automaton** (`.cyclic`) is David Griffeath's rule, with the threshold-range generalization from Robert Fisch, Janko Gravner & David Griffeath, ["Threshold-range scaling of excitable cellular automata"](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01890834) (*Statistics and Computing* 1, 1991); the **excitable medium** (`.excitable`) is the Greenberg-Hastings model (James M. Greenberg & Stuart P. Hastings, "Spatial patterns for discrete models of diffusion in excitable media", *SIAM Journal on Applied Mathematics* 34/3, 1978), in the same threshold-range form; **Brian's Brain** (`.briansBrain`) is Brian Silverman's three-state rule; and the **hodgepodge machine** (`.hodgepodge`) is Martin Gerhardt & Heike Schuster's model of a catalytic oscillating reaction ("A cellular automaton describing the formation of spatially ordered structures in chemical systems", *Physica D* 36, 1989), implemented in the formulation A. K. Dewdney spelled out in "The hodgepodge machine makes waves" (*Scientific American* 259/2, Computer Recreations, August 1988): the healthy cell's ⌊A/k1⌋ + ⌊B/k2⌋ catch, the infected cell's neighborhood-average climb plus g, and the instant recovery at the top state. All four run as fragment passes over the persistent ping-pong layer, written independently.

- The **fluid** simulation (`.fluid`, a real-time incompressible flow that carries color) reimplements the stable-fluids method, written from the technique: semi-Lagrangian advection and the Jacobi pressure-projection that keeps the flow divergence-free are Jos Stam's ["Stable Fluids"](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/people/stam/reality/Research/pdf/ns.pdf) (1999), realized on the GPU after Mark Harris' ["Fast Fluid Dynamics Simulation on the GPU"](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems/part-vi-beyond-triangles/chapter-38-fast-fluid-dynamics-simulation-gpu) (*GPU Gems* 38); the **vorticity confinement** that restores swirling detail is from Fedkiw, Stam & Jensen's ["Visual Simulation of Smoke"](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs237d/smoke.pdf) (2001). The splat-driven real-time arrangement and parameter feel were studied from Pavel Dobryakov's [WebGL-Fluid-Simulation](https://github.com/PavelDoGreat/WebGL-Fluid-Simulation) (MIT). Written independently.
- The **artificial-life** systems (`ParticleLife`, `PPS`, `Physarum`) and the GPU neighbor search under them each reimplement a published technique, written from the paper or article. **Particle Life** (a few particle kinds and a per-ordered-pair attraction/repulsion matrix, from which membranes, cells, and chasers emerge) is Jeffrey Ventrella's *Clusters*, popularized as "particle life"; its short-range-repulsion-then-attraction force follows [Tom Mohr's particle-life](https://github.com/tom-mohr/particle-life) parameterization, cross-read against [lisyarus' WebGPU write-up](https://lisyarus.github.io/blog/posts/particle-life-simulation-in-browser-using-webgpu.html). The **Primordial Particle System** (one turning rule, `Δφ = α + β·N·sign(R − L)`, that grows dividing cells) is Thomas Schmickl, Martin Stefanec and Karl Crailsheim's ["How a life-like system emerges from a simplistic particle motion law"](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep37969) (*Scientific Reports* 6, 37969, 2016), with the paper's canonical `⟨r = 5, α = 180°, β = 17°, v = 0.67⟩`. **Physarum** transport networks (agents that sense three points ahead, steer toward the strongest, deposit a trail, and let it diffuse and decay) are Jeff Jones' agent model, ["Characteristics of Pattern Formation and Evolution in Approximations of Physarum Transport Networks"](https://doi.org/10.1162/artl.2010.16.2.16202) (*Artificial Life* 16(2), 2010). **Particle Lenia** (particles that walk down the gradient of an energy field `E = R − G(U)`, where a ring-shaped kernel summed over neighbors gives the field `U`, a growth function scores it, and a repulsion term keeps particles off each other) is Alexander Mordvintsev, Eyvind Niklasson and Ettore Randazzo's ["Particle Lenia and the energy-based formulation"](https://google-research.github.io/self-organising-systems/particle-lenia/) (Google Research, Self-Organising Systems, 2022), itself a particle rewriting of Bert Wang-Chak Chan's Lenia. Ollin derives the kernel's weight rather than tabulating it, since it is the constant that normalizes the kernel over the plane. **Swarm chemistry** (particles each carrying their own eight-value kinetic recipe, with Boids-style cohesion, alignment, separation, random steering and pace-keeping) is Hiroki Sayama's ["Swarm Chemistry"](https://doi.org/10.1162/artl.2009.15.1.15107) (*Artificial Life* 15(1), 2009); the evolutionary layer, where recipes transmit between colliding particles with mutation and a competition function decides the direction of transfer, follows his later work on open-ended evolution in the model, including ["Seeking Open-Ended Evolution in Swarm Chemistry II"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.03304) (Sayama, 2018). The published parameter table and rule list were read from Sayama's ["Guiding Designs of Self-Organizing Swarms"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1308.3400); Ollin converts recipes out of that model's own length scale into whatever world a sketch gives it, so a shared recipe means the same behavior at any canvas size.

The GPU **uniform-grid fixed-radius neighbor search** they share (`SpatialHash`, a counting sort: count into cells, prefix-sum into offsets, scatter into slots) follows Rama Hoetzlein's ["Fast Fixed-Radius Nearest Neighbors: Interactive Million-Particle Fluids"](https://ramakarl.com/fluids3/) (NVIDIA, GTC 2014). The open-ended-evolution direction these point toward is explored at scale by the [ALIEN](https://github.com/chrxh/alien) project (Christian Heinemann), whose license is BSD-3-Clause; it remains an inspiration only, studied for which techniques exist rather than for its code, which is CUDA. No code was taken from any of these; all written independently in Swift and Metal.
- The GPU **particle fluid and soft bodies** (`ParticleFluid`, `SoftBodies`) reimplement three published papers over the `SpatialHash` neighbor search. The fluid is smoothed-particle hydrodynamics after Müller, Charypar & Gross, ["Particle-Based Fluid Simulation for Interactive Applications"](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/sca03.pdf) (SCA 2003): density summation, the symmetrized pressure force with a non-vanishing ("spiky"-family) gradient so crowded particles always separate, and the `p = k(ρ − ρ0)` state equation, with the kernels normalized for 2D by direct integration. Its anti-clustering **near-density / near-pressure** pair (which also produces the emergent surface tension) is from Clavet, Beaudoin & Poulin, ["Particle-based Viscoelastic Fluid Simulation"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1073368.1073400) (SCA 2005), whose quadratic/cubic spike kernels the fluid uses; evaluating all passes at short-lookahead predicted positions is standard practice in interactive GPU SPH (cross-read against Sebastian Lague's [Fluid-Sim](https://github.com/SebLague/Fluid-Sim), MIT, and Lucas Schuermann's [mueller-sph](https://github.com/cerrno/mueller-sph), MIT). The soft bodies are meshless **shape matching** after Müller, Heidelberger, Teschner & Gross, ["Meshless Deformations Based on Shape Matching"](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/MeshlessDeformations_SIG05.pdf) (SIGGRAPH 2005): goal positions from the closed-form 2D best-fit rotation of rest offsets about the rest centroid, pulled at a single unconditionally-stable stiffness. No code was taken from any of these; all written independently in Swift and Metal.
- The shader library's hashing, value noise, `curlNoise`, and disc sampling (spliced for free into every user shader and `ComputeKernel`) reimplement published techniques in Metal: Dave Hoskins' [Hash without Sine](https://www.shadertoy.com/view/4djSRW), the value-noise lattice from [The Book of Shaders](https://thebookofshaders.com/11/), and the curl-of-a-noise-potential construction, written independently.
- The noise toolkit (`simplexNoise`, `worley`, `ridgedFbm`, `turbulence`, and `warpedFbm`, each a CPU helper and a shader-library function, plus the `.cellular` generator and the `warp` knob on `.noise`) reimplements the published techniques on both sides: **simplex noise** is Ken Perlin's simplex construction (*Real-Time Shading* SIGGRAPH 2001 course; his hardware-noise patent expired in 2022), implemented from Stefan Gustavson's ["Simplex noise demystified"](https://cgvr.cs.uni-bremen.de/teaching/cg_literatur/simplexnoise.pdf) (2005), with Ollin's own contrast calibration; **Worley cellular noise** is Steven Worley's texture basis ("A Cellular Texture Basis Function", SIGGRAPH 1996), in the cell-space 3×3 scan form taught by [The Book of Shaders' cellular-noise chapter](https://thebookofshaders.com/12/), the animated generator wandering its feature points the way that chapter does; **ridged fbm** is F. Kenton Musgrave's ridged multifractal (*Texturing & Modeling: A Procedural Approach*, and his published `musgrave.c`: the folded-and-squared signal with its octave-weight feedback), reparameterized onto `fbm`'s gain/lacunarity knobs; **turbulence** is Ken Perlin's fold ("An Image Synthesizer", SIGGRAPH 1985); and **domain warping** follows [Inigo Quilez's warp article](https://iquilezles.org/articles/warp/) (the two-level fbm displacement with his offsets, exposed one-call as `warpedFbm` and opened up in the `DomainWarp` example). All written independently in Swift and Metal.
- The escape-time fractal `Generator`s render the sets of Gaston Julia (1918) and Benoit Mandelbrot (1980), colored by the standard continuous ("smooth") iteration count (the renormalized escape time, `n + 1 - log2(log2|z|)`), written from the published mathematics.
- The `.orbitTrap` generator colors the same iteration by the orbit's minimum distance to a trap shape, a technique introduced by Clifford Pickover (his cross is the "Pickover stalks"). The epsilon-cross form dates to Fractint, 1989. Cross-read against [Inigo Quilez's geometric orbit traps](https://iquilezles.org/articles/ftrapsgeometric/), then written independently.
- The `.domainColoring` generator paints a complex function over its own plane, coloring each point by the direction of the value there. The method and its name come from Frank A. Farris (his review of Tristan Needham's *Visual Complex Analysis*, *American Mathematical Monthly* 105, 1998, where the term "domain coloring" is introduced), and the shading forms follow Elias Wegert's phase portraits (*Visual Complex Functions: An Introduction with Phase Portraits*, Birkhauser, 2012): the plain portrait, the modulus ruling that ramps between successive doublings of the value, and the two rulings together, which draw the conformal grid a holomorphic function carries. That the wheels around a zero and a pole run opposite ways, and count multiplicity, is the argument principle. Written from the published technique.
- The `.diffuse` filter and `drawDiffusionCurve` implement **diffusion curves** (Alexandrina Orzan, Adrien Bousseau, Holger Winnemoller, Pascal Barla, Joelle Thollot, and David Salesin, ["Diffusion Curves: A Vector Representation for Smooth-Shaded Images"](https://doi.org/10.1145/1360612.1360691), *ACM Transactions on Graphics* 27(3), SIGGRAPH 2008): curves carrying a color on each side, and the image as the solution of the Laplace equation those colors constrain, solved coarse to fine. Ollin's solve is its own: the constraints are the layer's own drawn pixels rather than rasterized curve geometry, they ride down the pyramid in premultiplied form so a hairline mark survives the coarse sizes, and a partly covered texel pulls in proportion to its coverage rather than being held. Written from the paper.
- The `.seamlessClone` combine implements **Poisson image editing** (Patrick Perez, Michel Gangnet, and Andrew Blake, ["Poisson Image Editing"](https://doi.org/10.1145/882262.882269), *ACM Transactions on Graphics* 22(3), SIGGRAPH 2003): a region pasted into another image so the seam vanishes, by solving for the field whose gradients match the source and whose values match the destination around the region's boundary. Ollin uses the paper's membrane form of that solve, where the correction added to the patch is the harmonic function matching the destination-minus-source difference around the rim. The field itself is settled with a **convolution pyramid** (Zeev Farbman, Raanan Fattal, and Dani Lischinski, ["Convolution Pyramids"](https://doi.org/10.1145/2070781.2024209), *ACM Transactions on Graphics* 30(6), SIGGRAPH Asia 2011), which stands in for one very wide kernel with a multiscale scheme of three small fitted kernels, in one pass down the sizes and one back up rather than by relaxation; the fitted membrane weights are that paper's published values. Both written from the papers, independently. OPENRNDR's [orx-poisson-fill](https://github.com/openrndr/orx) was cross-read beforehand for which pieces of this family exist and how they are approached, the same way LYGIA and ofxFX inform the filter catalog. The region is taken from the patch layer's own alpha, and the float32 intermediates (the fitted kernels are unnormalized, so a layer's worth of levels leaves half float's range at both ends), the rim extraction, and the composite are Ollin's own.
- The pattern-field `Generator`s reimplement classic closed-form constructions, each written from the published form: **`.quasicrystal`** is the summed-plane-wave quasicrystal (waves at evenly spaced angles), the construction popularized by [Keegan McAllister's "Quasicrystals as sums of waves in the plane"](http://mainisusuallyafunction.blogspot.com/2011/10/quasicrystals-as-sums-of-waves-in-plane.html) (2011), whose mathematical roots are de Bruijn's projection theory of Penrose tilings; **`.gyroid`** slices Alan Schoen's gyroid (the triply periodic minimal surface he described in 1970, NASA TN D-5541) by its standard level-set approximation; **`.phyllotaxis`** evaluates Helmut Vogel's sunflower model (*A better way to construct the sunflower head*, 1979) as a per-pixel nearest-floret field; **`.moire`** is textbook two-grating moiré interference; and **`.hexPulse`** rides the standard two-lattice modulo construction for hexagonal tiling common in the shader community. All five written independently in Metal.
- The design-pattern `Generator` catalog (`.meshGradient`, `.filaments`, `.smokeRing`, `.colorPanels`, `.spiral`, `.waves`, `.dotOrbit`, `.grainGradient`, `.pulsingBorder`, `.godRays`) was cross-read against [Paper Shaders](https://github.com/paper-design/shaders) (paper.design, Apache-2.0; shaders by Ksenia Kondrashova) for which design-shader effects exist and how they're approached (the same way LYGIA and ofxFX inform the filter catalog), then each was written independently in Metal from the underlying published technique: the **mesh gradient** blends orbiting color points by inverse-distance weighting ([Shepard's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_distance_weighting), 1968) over [Inigo Quilez domain warping](https://iquilezles.org/articles/warp/); the **filaments** pattern is the cross-octave sine-feedback fractal from [@zozuar's one-tweet shader](https://x.com/zozuar/status/1625182758745128981), popularized by Kondrashova's "Neuro Noise"; **smokeRing** and **godRays** are polar-domain value-noise fields (with the standard double-parameterization blend hiding the atan2 seam); **colorPanels** is an analytic ray/plane intersection fan; **spiral** is the classic `fract(r + θ/2π)` level set with derivative-based anti-aliasing; **dotOrbit** animates the feature points of Worley cellular noise; **grainGradient** bands a scalar field (its `.truchet` field after the Smith quarter-arc tile credited below, its `.blob` the metaball falloff sum) with noise added to the field *before* quantization; and **pulsingBorder** rides Quilez's rounded-box SDF with angular sector masks. Palette banding, premultiplied layer compositing, and the sRGB-space blending that keeps designer palettes true are shared plumbing across the set. The design-filter siblings (`.flutedGlass`, `.water`, `.paperTexture`, `.liquidMetal`, `.heatmap`, `.gemSmoke`) were cross-read against the same catalog and written from the canonical techniques likewise: **fluted glass** is per-cell 1D refraction (quantize into strips, displace each strip's sampling coordinate by a fixed profile, the standard screen-space approximation of a cylindrical lens array); **water**'s caustics are the iterated domain-rotated sine accumulation (the community-standard tileable-caustic construction, luminance from the derivatives of a phase-warped field), over screen-space refraction offsets; **paper texture** is finite-difference bump lighting (Blinn) of a height field built from value-noise tooth, Worley crumple facets and speckles, and fibers extracted as the gradient magnitude of a domain-rotated fbm; **liquid metal** is a 1D chrome reflectance ramp (two hot hairlines plus a broad sky-to-ground gradient per cycle, the airbrush-chrome idiom whose 2D ancestor is the lit-sphere/matcap model of Sloan et al., "The Lit Sphere", GI 2001) indexed by a warped coordinate whose curvature proxy is a blurred interior field of the shape's alpha; **heatmap** and **gem smoke** shade multi-radius blurred alpha fields through the same palette walks. The liquid-metal/gem-smoke interior-inflation field (the Poisson "pillow" problem the originals solve on the CPU per logo) is solved on the GPU as coarse-to-fine Jacobi relaxation fragment passes, so it runs per frame over animated layers; heatmap's multi-radius blurred fields match its reference, which is blur-based. The **liquid metal** band pattern and **fluted glass** refraction follow Paper Shaders' published parameterization closely (the per-cycle band layout with its wide gradient, the hand-shaped per-channel dispersion, and the per-profile border fades are their design, reexpressed in Ollin's Metal over Ollin's field prep); Apache-2.0 permits this freely, and the debt is recorded here.
- The fluent `Visual` chain tier (compose sources, warps, color moves, blends, and modulations into one generated fragment shader) borrows its *composition model* from [Hydra](https://github.com/ojack/hydra) (Olivia Jack): the closed algebra of source / coordinate / color / combine / modulate operations, the pullback trick of warping the sampling coordinate rather than the image, and modulation as one chain's color driving another's coordinates, the analog-video-synth idea. Hydra is AGPL-3.0, so its architecture was studied and described, never its source translated; every operation is Ollin's own Metal, and the parameter model differs deliberately (values ride a uniform buffer keyed off the chain's structure, so animation never recompiles). The value-type staged-trace approach (the host language records the chain; codegen follows) was studied from [Shader Park](https://github.com/shader-park) (MIT). The op implementations are standard published techniques: the polar regular-polygon distance from [The Book of Shaders' shapes chapter](https://thebookofshaders.com/07/), the hexcone RGB↔HSV conversions, the jittered-lattice cellular pattern after [Inigo Quilez's Voronoi articles](https://iquilezles.org/articles/smoothvoronoi/), and the hash/value-noise lattice already credited above. Written independently.
- The GLSL shader importer (`ollin new --from-shader`) translates the fragment-shader shape [Shadertoy](https://www.shadertoy.com) established (Inigo Quilez and Pol Jeremias): a `mainImage(out vec4 fragColor, in vec2 fragCoord)` entry point over the `iTime` / `iResolution` / `iMouse` / `iFrame` / `iChannel0-3` values the site supplies each frame. That contract is read from the site's own published documentation and mapped onto Ollin's `shade(uv, info)`; no shader is bundled, and the importer only ever translates source the user hands it, with the author's terms recorded in the emitted file's header (much of the public corpus is CC BY-NC-SA or reserved, so this matters). Shaders are fetched, when asked, only through the site's own public API with a key the user supplies. The translation itself is Ollin's own lexer and rewriter, written from the two language specifications rather than from any existing translator; where a Metal built-in is not a drop-in replacement it writes the GLSL form out instead, most importantly `mod`, whose GLSL definition floors the quotient (`x - y*floor(x/y)`) where Metal's `fmod` truncates it, so the two disagree for negative operands. That difference is long documented in the porting literature; it was cross-checked against [naga issue #802](https://github.com/gfx-rs/naga/issues/802) and the [SDFMetalDemo notes on porting `mod`](https://github.com/novocodev/SDFMetalDemo/wiki/Porting-GLSL-shader-mod()-function-to-Metal-Compute-Shader).
- The OllinLiveCoding performance host follows the livecoding performance model of [Hydra](https://github.com/ojack/hydra) (Olivia Jack): the code is drawn over the running visuals as part of the show, edits evaluate into the running piece without stopping it, and hiding the code turns the same window into pure projection. Hydra is AGPL-3.0, so the model was studied as a product and never its source translated; the host is Ollin's own SwiftUI/AppKit app over Ollin's own dylib hot-swap engine, and where Hydra evaluates JavaScript per keystroke, Swift compiles, so the loop is deliberately evaluate-on-command.

### Generative geometry

- `curlNoise` follows the curl-noise method for divergence-free flow (Robert Bridson and colleagues, "Curl-Noise for Procedural Fluid Flow", 2007).
- The `poissonDisk` blue-noise sampler follows Robert Bridson's ["Fast Poisson Disk Sampling in Arbitrary Dimensions"](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rbridson/docs/bridson-siggraph07-poissondisk.pdf), 2007 (the background-grid dart-throwing that makes it linear-time).
- The `haltonPoints` low-discrepancy sequence is John H. Halton's radical-inverse construction ("On the efficiency of certain quasi-random sequences of points in evaluating multi-dimensional integrals", *Numerische Mathematik* 2, 1960); `sobolPoints` is Ilya M. Sobol's sequence (1967) generated with the standard Gray-code stepping (I. A. Antonov and V. M. Saleev, 1979; Paul Bratley and Bennett L. Fox, "Algorithm 659", 1988), the direction numbers for its second dimension from the degree-one primitive-polynomial recurrence as tabulated by [Stephen Joe and Frances Y. Kuo](https://web.maths.unsw.edu.au/~fkuo/sobol/). Written from the technique.
- `surfacePoints` scatters a mesh's surface by three published pieces, each written from the technique. A triangle is chosen in proportion to its area from a running-total table, and the point inside it comes from [Eric Heitz's low-distortion square-to-triangle map](https://eheitzresearch.wordpress.com/749-2/) (Unity Technologies technical report, 2019), which presses the square's two halves together across the diagonal instead of taking the square root the classic map uses (Greg Turk, "Generating Random Points in Triangles", *Graphics Gems*, 1990, as used for shape sampling by Robert Osada, Thomas Funkhouser, Bernard Chazelle and David Dobkin, "Shape Distributions", *ACM TOG* 21(4), 2002), so it costs no square root and keeps an even input even. The `.blueNoise` spread thins an oversampled draw by the weighted sample elimination of [Cem Yuksel](https://www.cemyuksel.com/research/sampleelimination/) ("Sample Elimination for Generating Poisson Disk Sample Sets", *Computer Graphics Forum* 34(2), 2015): a weight per sample that falls to nothing at the honeycomb spacing the count deserves, the heaviest eliminated repeatedly, which is what lets a blue-noise set be asked for by count on a surface where a radius has no obvious value.
- `stipple` implements Adrian Secord's ["Weighted Voronoi Stippling"](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/imager/tr/2002/secord2002b/secord.2002b.pdf) (NPAR 2002): dots seeded by rejection-sampling an image's darkness, then iterated to the darkness-weighted centroids of their Voronoi regions (here via exact nearest-dot pixel assignment over a working grid). Written from the paper.
- The random-walk family: `randomWalk` is the classic isotropic walk (the name from Karl Pearson's 1905 *Nature* letter); `levyFlight` draws step lengths from a truncated power law by inverse-CDF sampling, the heavy-tailed walk Benoit Mandelbrot named the Lévy flight (after Paul Lévy) in *The Fractal Geometry of Nature*; `selfAvoidingWalk` grows the classic lattice self-avoiding walk depth-first with backtracking so it winds long instead of trapping early. Written from the techniques.
- The `truchet` tiling places one tile per grid cell at a random orientation, a pattern named for Sébastien Truchet (1704); the `.arcs` tile with its quarter-circles joining edge midpoints is the variant Cyril Stanley Smith described in ["The Tiling Patterns of Sebastian Truchet and the Topology of Structural Hierarchy"](https://doi.org/10.1016/0024-3795(87)90153-9), 1987.
- `hitomezashi` renders the one-stitch sashiko designs of Japanese embroidery from one phase bit per grid line, the binary specification (and the two-colorability its `parities` implements) formalized by Katherine A. Seaton and Carol Hayes in ["Mathematical specification of hitomezashi designs"](https://doi.org/10.1080/17513472.2023.2187999) (*Journal of Mathematics and the Arts* 17, 2023). Written from the paper.
- `kolam` walks the single line of the south Indian **kolam** (the looped *sikku*/*kambi* kind, drawn around a grid of pulli) and the Angolan **sona** drawn in sand, which are the same object: a 45-degree path that reflects off the edges of a dot field and closes on itself. The two traditions were set side by side by Marcia Ascher (*Ethnomathematics: A Multicultural View of Mathematical Ideas*, Brooks/Cole, 1991), and the sona were recorded and analyzed at length by Paulus Gerdes (*Sona Geometry from Angola: Mathematics of an African Tradition*, Polimetrica, 2006), whose account carries the loop count: a plain field of `m` by `n` dots closes into gcd(m, n) loops. The general object, and the wall (a mirror placed between two dots, each one splitting or joining exactly one loop) is Slavik Jablan's mirror-curve construction ("Mirror generated curves", *Symmetry: Culture and Science* 6, 1995). Written from the published descriptions; the traditions themselves are credited to the people who keep them, not to the papers.
- `Pursuit` implements the classical curve of pursuit: a runner heading always at the current position of the one it follows. Pierre Bouguer first studied it in 1732, in ["Sur de nouvelles courbes ausquelles on peut donner le nom de lignes de poursuite"](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/item/88479) (*Memoires de l'Academie Royale des Sciences*), written about one ship pursuing another. The ring case, where runners on the corners of a regular polygon each chase the next, was posed by Edouard Lucas in 1877 and answered by Henri Brocard: the paths are logarithmic spirals meeting in one point. The path length `1 / (1 - cos(2 pi / n))` for a unit side, the constant angle to the center, and the straight-line (radiodrome) results are the standard published ones, gathered in [Wolfram MathWorld's "Mice Problem"](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/MiceProblem.html) and ["Pursuit Curve"](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/PursuitCurve.html). Written from those results; the stepper, the simultaneous update, the stride floor under the catch distance, and the kept chase lines are this implementation's own.
- `Billiard` implements dynamical billiards, the study of a point bouncing in a region, which goes back to a question of Birkhoff and is surveyed in Serge Tabachnikov's [*Geometry and Billiards*](https://www.personal.psu.edu/sot2/books/billiardsgeometry.pdf) (American Mathematical Society, 2005). Three published results are what the tests measure rather than the pictures: in a circle the distance from the center to every chord is conserved, so the path stays tangent to one inner caustic; in an ellipse the product of the distances from the two foci to a chord is conserved (the Joachimsthal integral), which is why an ellipse sorts paths into those that pass between the foci and those that do not; and the stadium of Leonid Bunimovich, ["On the ergodic properties of nowhere dispersing billiards"](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01197884) (*Communications in Mathematical Physics* 65, 1979), is chaotic despite being built only from flats and arcs, as is Yakov Sinai's square with a round obstacle in it (1970). Written from those published results, not from any implementation of them: the room cases, the intersections, the reflection at each wall, and the `Table` and path surfaces are this implementation's own.
- `Drainage` implements the standard raster-hydrology chain. Flow direction is the D8 method of John O'Callaghan and David Mark, ["The extraction of drainage networks from digital elevation data"](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0734-189X(84)80011-0) (*Computer Vision, Graphics, and Image Processing* 28, 1984), with the slope to a diagonal neighbor taken over its own longer distance. Depression filling is the Priority-Flood algorithm of Richard Barnes, Clarence Lehman, and David Mulla, ["Priority-flood: An optimal depression-filling and watershed-labeling algorithm for digital elevation models"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.04463) (*Computers & Geosciences* 62, 2014), in its epsilon variant, which keeps a hair of slope over the cell that flooded each one. Stream ordering is Arthur Strahler's, ["Quantitative analysis of watershed geomorphology"](https://doi.org/10.1029/TR038i006p00913) (*Transactions of the American Geophysical Union* 38, 1957), which refined Robert Horton's earlier scheme. Written from those published results, not from any implementation of them: the flow counting, the routing of filled hollows by the flood's own parent link, the basin labeling, the tracing of reaches, and the `River` surface are this implementation's own.
- `Anamorphosis` implements catoptric (mirror) anamorphosis for a cylindrical mirror. The technique predates the mathematics that describes it: Renaissance workshops ruled the construction out by hand, and Jean-Francois Niceron set it down in *La Perspective Curieuse* (1638) ([Wikipedia, "Anamorphosis"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anamorphosis); [Wikipedia, "Jean-Francois Niceron"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Fran%C3%A7ois_Nic%C3%A9ron)). The map here is the modern ray construction: take the ray from the eye to a point on the cylinder, reflect it about the surface normal there, and follow it to the page, the construction set out for a standing cylindrical mirror by Aleksandar Cucakovic and Magdalena Paunovic in ["Cylindrical Mirror Anamorphosis and Urban-Architectural Ambience"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0239-7) (*Nexus Network Journal* 17, 2015). The map back is [Alhazen's problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhazen%27s_problem), posed by Ibn al-Haytham around 1025, which has no elementary solution; it is solved here by a scan-and-bisect search over the arc both ends can see, and the bounce height then follows from the reflection keeping the path's fall. Written from those published results, not from any implementation of them: the true-size wrap, the visibility limits, the contour and shape handling, and the search are this implementation's own.
- `Clothoid` implements the Euler spiral, the curve whose curvature is a linear function of arc length. Leonhard Euler described it in 1744 and Augustin-Jean Fresnel arrived at the same integrals in his work on diffraction, which is why the position is written in the Fresnel integrals `C(t)` and `S(t)` ([Wolfram MathWorld, "Cornu Spiral"](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/CornuSpiral.html); [Wikipedia, "Euler spiral"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_spiral)). Marie Alfred Cornu plotted it, Ernesto Cesaro named it the clothoid, and Arthur Talbot brought it into railway practice in 1890 as the transition curve, which is the easement and corner-rounding use here ([Wikipedia, "Track transition curve"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_transition_curve)). The `G1` fit that joins two points with two headings follows the reduction published by Enrico Bertolazzi and Marco Frego in ["Fast and accurate G1 fitting of clothoid curves"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1305.6644) (2013; *Mathematical Methods in the Applied Sciences*, 2015), which collapses three nonlinear equations to the single zero of one function and supplies the initial guess. Written from those published results, not from any implementation of them: the quadrature (composite Gauss-Legendre rather than the paper's Fresnel momenta with their small-parameter expansions), the corner fitting and its one-step rescale, the spline, and the chain readers are this implementation's own.
- `CreasePattern`, `MiuraFold`, and `RotatingSquares` implement published origami and kirigami results. The two flat-folding laws are [Kawasaki's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kawasaki%27s_theorem) (the alternating sum of the angles around a vertex is zero; Toshikazu Kawasaki, 1989, arrived at independently by Jacques Justin and by Robert Hull) and [Maekawa's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maekawa%27s_theorem) (the mountains and valleys at a vertex differ by two; attributed to Jun Maekawa, published by Kazuo Haga). The fold itself is the Miura-ori, devised by Koryo Miura in 1970 and published as ["Method of Packaging and Deployment of Large Membranes in Space"](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ISASR...618....1M) (*Institute of Space and Astronautical Science Report* 618, 1985); its rigid folding, its auxetic behavior, and the reciprocal pair of Poisson's ratios are the standard results, gathered in Mark Schenk and Simon Guest, ["Geometry of Miura-folded metamaterials"](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1217998110) (*PNAS* 110(9), 2013). The cut sheet is the rotating-squares auxetic of Joseph Grima and Kenneth Evans, ["Auxetic behavior from rotating squares"](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1006781224002) (*Journal of Materials Science Letters* 19, 2000), whose Poisson's ratio of exactly -1 is their result. Written from those published results: the folded-state parameterization used here (one lattice line under three isometry constraints), the derivation of the mountain and valley marks from the folded geometry, the vertex matching, and the joining of creases into pen strokes are this implementation's own.
- `ShapeGrammar` implements shape grammars, introduced by George Stiny and James Gips in ["Shape Grammars and the Generative Specification of Painting and Sculpture"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221329330) (*Information Processing 71*, IFIP Congress, 1971), with the formal apparatus of labelled shapes and rule application under a transformation set out in Stiny's *Pictorial and Formal Aspects of Shape and Shape Grammars* (Birkhauser, 1975). The lattice grammar follows George Stiny, ["Ice-ray: a note on the generation of Chinese lattice designs"](https://doi.org/10.1068/b040089) (*Environment and Planning B* 4(1), 1977), whose parametric rules cut a polygon in two along a line drawn between two of its edges, leaving parts of approximately equal area and stopping below a size constant; that paper was written from Daniel Sheets Dye's *A Grammar of Chinese Lattice* (Harvard University Press, 1949), the catalogue of the lattices themselves. The split rules are the same idea applied to buildings, after Peter Wonka, Michael Wimmer, Francois Sillion and William Ribarsky, ["Instant Architecture"](https://doi.org/10.1145/882262.882324) (*ACM Transactions on Graphics* 22(3), 2003) and Pascal Muller, Peter Wonka, Simon Haegler, Andreas Ulmer and Luc Van Gool, ["Procedural Modeling of Buildings"](https://doi.org/10.1145/1141911.1141931) (*ACM Transactions on Graphics* 25(3), 2006). Written from those published rules: the closed-form solve for the balanced cut, the derivation of the whole ice-ray rule table from a corner range, the shortest-of-N cut choice, the mitered inset and its collapse test, and the weight-driven rule choice are this implementation's own.
- `knotwork` weaves the same walk as bands, which is the plait-and-breakline construction behind Celtic interlace: a plait of diagonal cords, walls (breaklines) placed between dots to shape it, and an over/under choice that alternates along every cord. - The `HexGrid` lattice math (axial and offset coordinates, the hex-to-pixel mapping and its cube-rounded inverse, and the neighbor, distance, and ring walks) follows Amit Patel's [Hexagonal Grids guide](https://www.redblobgames.com/grids/hexagons/) (Red Blob Games), the standard reference for hex-grid geometry. `TriangleGrid` uses the standard alternating up/down strip construction, cross-checked against [Boris the Brave's triangle-grids article](https://www.boristhebrave.com/2021/05/23/triangle-grids/). Written from the math.
- `Subdivision` implements the folk recipe behind recursive "grid painting" compositions (an aspect-aware binary cut at a bounded random fraction, with minimum-size, depth, and probability stops, plus the probabilistic quadtree variant); the recipe is community folklore with no single canonical source. Its rigorous relative is the Mondrian process (Daniel M. Roy and Yee Whye Teh, ["The Mondrian Process"](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/3622-the-mondrian-process), NIPS 2008), and Loe Feijs's analyses ("Divisions of the Plane by Computer", *Leonardo* 37(3), 2004; [arXiv:2011.00843](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00843)) are the honest footnote that recursive splitting produces the look, not the painter's actual method.
- The `Maze` generators implement the classic algorithms from their published descriptions: the randomized depth-first "recursive backtracker", randomized Kruskal's (Joseph B. Kruskal, "On the Shortest Spanning Subtree of a Graph and the Traveling Salesman Problem", *Proc. The survey that maps this territory is Jamis Buck's [maze-generation series](https://weblog.jamisbuck.org/2011/2/7/maze-generation-algorithm-recap) and his *Mazes for Programmers* (Pragmatic Bookshelf, 2015); the longest path is the standard double-BFS tree diameter. Written from the algorithms. (The famous one-line BASIC maze *look*, random diagonals rather than carved walls, is the Truchet `.diagonals` tile above; Nick Montfort et al., *10 PRINT CHR$(205.5+RND(1)); : GOTO 10*, MIT Press, 2012.)
- `apollonianGasket` fills a circle by the Descartes circle theorem (stated by René Descartes in his 1643 letters to Princess Elisabeth of the Palatinate; Frederick Soddy's poem "The Kiss Precise", *Nature*, 1936), with each circle's center from the theorem's complex extension (Jeffrey C. Lagarias, Colin L. Mallows, and Allan R. Wilks, ["Beyond the Descartes Circle Theorem"](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0101066), *American Mathematical Monthly* 109, 2002). Each new circle is the Descartes quadratic's other root given its parent triple (Vieta's formulas), so the recursion is a linear jump with no square roots. Written from the theorem.
- `concaveHull` implements the **characteristic shape** ("chi-shape") of Matt Duckham, Lars Kulik, Mike Worboys, and Antony Galton (["Efficient generation of simple polygons for characterizing the shape of a set of points in the plane"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patcog.2008.03.023), *Pattern Recognition* 41(10), 2008): border triangles of the Delaunay triangulation eroded longest-boundary-edge-first, blocked whenever the opposite vertex already sits on the boundary, which is what keeps the polygon simple and every input point inside; the scale-free 0...1 knob interpolates the length threshold between the triangulation's shortest and longest edge. Written from the paper.
- `alphaShape` implements the **alpha shape** of Herbert Edelsbrunner, David G. Kirkpatrick, and Raimund Seidel ("On the Shape of a Set of Points in the Plane", *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory* 29(4), 1983), in its standard alpha-complex form: keep the Delaunay triangles whose circumradius is at most alpha, and the boundary (the edges owned by exactly one kept triangle) linked into island and hole contours. Written from the technique.
- `medialAxis` approximates **Blum's medial axis** (Harry Blum, "A Transformation for Extracting New Descriptors of Shape", in *Models for the Perception of Speech and Visual Form*, MIT Press, 1967) by the standard **Voronoi subcomplex of a boundary sampling** (J. W. Brandt and V. R. Algazi, "Continuous skeleton computation by Voronoi diagram", *CVGIP: Image Understanding* 55(3), 1992): the Voronoi edges of evenly resampled boundary points whose generating samples aren't neighbors along the contour and whose endpoints stay inside the region, with each skeleton vertex carrying its circumradius as the inscribed-disk clearance. Written from the papers.
- `straightSkeleton` implements the **straight skeleton** of Oswin Aichholzer, Franz Aurenhammer, David Alberts, and Bernd Gärtner (["A novel type of skeleton for polygons"](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-80350-5_65), *Journal of Universal Computer Science* 1(12), 1995), by the wavefront event-queue formulation of Petr Felkel and Štěpán Obdržálek ("Straight Skeleton Implementation", *SCCG* 1998) hardened the way modern implementations are: simultaneous events are clustered by level and meeting point and processed as one generalized event over chains of wavefront edges, the treatment introduced by Tom Kelly (*Unwritten procedural modeling with the straight skeleton*, PhD thesis, University of Glasgow, 2013) and studied here in Tomasz Kędziora's kendzi-straight-skeleton (BSD-3) and Kelly's campskeleton (Apache-2.0), both read for structure, neither translated. Mitered insets cut each skeleton face as the roof plane it is (Peter Palfrader and Martin Held, ["Computing Mitered Offset Curves Based on Straight Skeletons"](https://doi.org/10.1080/16864360.2014.997637), *Computer-Aided Design and Applications* 12(4), 2015). Written from the publications, with our own face assembly (skeleton nodes shared by identity, faces stitched from each edge's boundary arcs).
- `Marbling` implements **mathematical paper marbling** in Aubrey Jaffer's closed-form model ([Mathematical Marbling](https://people.csail.mit.edu/jaffer/Marbling/); Shufang Lu, Aubrey Jaffer, Xiaogang Jin, Hanli Zhao, and Xiaoyang Mao, ["Mathematical Marbling"](https://doi.org/10.1109/MCG.2011.51), *IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications* 32(1), 2012): the area-preserving ink-drop displacement (a point at distance `d` from a drop of radius `r` lands at `√(d² + r²)`) and the stylus family whose displacement decays exponentially with distance from the tool (tine line, comb, circular tine, vortex), here with the exponential base reparameterized as a half-distance falloff knob. Written from the publications.
- `Watercolor` implements Tyler Hobbs' generative-watercolor recipe (["A Guide to Simulating Watercolor Paint with Generative Art"](https://www.tylerxhobbs.com/words/a-guide-to-simulating-watercolor-paint-with-generative-art); expanded in his talk ["How to Hack a Painting"](https://tylerxhobbs.com/essays/2020/how-to-hack-a-painting)): a base polygon deformed by recursive midpoint subdivision with Gaussian jumps, per-edge variance inherited with decay and jitter so some stretches bloom while others stay crisp, and the painted result stacked as dozens of low-opacity further-deformed layers; the granulating speckle-under-clip texture in the example follows his masking trick. Written from the essay.
- `chladni` and the `.chladni` generator implement the closed-form **Chladni plate** figure of [Paul Bourke's "Chladni Plate Mathematics"](https://paulbourke.net/geometry/chladni/) (2003): for a square plate driven at its center, the standing wave `cos(nπx)cos(mπy) − cos(mπx)cos(nπy)` whose zero set is the figure sand traces, generalized with the two mode amplitudes as free weights. The physical figures themselves are Ernst Chladni's (*Entdeckungen über die Theorie des Klanges*, 1787). Written from the formula.
- `Heightfield.diamondSquare` implements the **diamond-square** midpoint-displacement fractal of Alain Fournier, Don Fussell, and Loren Carpenter (["Computer Rendering of Stochastic Models"](https://doi.org/10.1145/358523.358553), *Communications of the ACM* 25(6), 1982), in the standard non-wrapping form (border midpoints average their three available neighbors), with the per-level amplitude decay exposed as the `roughness` knob. Written from the technique.
- `Erosion.hydraulic` implements the **particle-based hydraulic erosion** of Hans Theobald Beyer (["Implementation of a method for hydraulic erosion"](https://www.firespark.de/resources/downloads/implementation%20of%20a%20methode%20for%20hydraulic%20erosion.pdf), bachelor thesis, Technische Universität München, 2015): drops steered by an inertia-blended bilinear gradient, the slope-speed-water carry capacity, surplus deposition split bilinearly over the four surrounding samples, and erosion taken from a distance-weighted brush radius. One deliberate deviation: the speed update uses the energy form `v' = √(max(0, v² − Δh·g))` so downhill legs accelerate (the thesis's eq. 5.7 as printed slows a falling drop and can go imaginary). Written from the thesis.
- `Erosion.thermal` implements **thermal weathering** as introduced by F. Kenton Musgrave, Craig E. Kolb, and Robert S. Mace (["The Synthesis and Rendering of Eroded Fractal Terrains"](https://doi.org/10.1145/74334.74337), *SIGGRAPH '89*): material above a talus angle repeatedly shed to the lower neighbors in proportion to their excess, here applied as an order-free relaxation (all transfers gathered against the frozen field per iteration) so the pass is deterministic and mass-conserving. Written from the paper.
- `Sim.ripples` implements the classic **interactive-water height-field kernel**, the height/velocity form popularized by Evan Wallace's [WebGL Water](https://madebyevan.com/webgl-water/) ([source](https://github.com/evanw/webgl-water), MIT): velocity accelerated toward the four-neighbor height average, damped, then integrated into the height, with drops added to the height channel only; Ollin adds a soft absorbing rim at the borders and routes drawn marks through an add-to-height inject. Written from the technique.
- The parametric 3D shape generators are written from their published forms. `drawSupershape`/`Mesh.supershape` is Johan Gielis's superformula (["A generic geometric transformation that unifies a wide range of natural and abstract shapes"](https://doi.org/10.3732/ajb.90.3.333), *American Journal of Botany* 90, 2003), evaluated once per latitude and once per longitude and multiplied in the spherical product; `drawSuperellipsoid` is the superquadric surface of Alan Barr (["Superquadrics and Angle-Preserving Transformations"](https://doi.org/10.1109/MCG.1981.1673799), *IEEE CG&A*, 1981), itself descended from Piet Hein's superellipse. `drawLathe` (surface of revolution) and `drawExtrude` (linear sweep of a plane figure) are standard constructive-geometry operations with no single source; the torus knot follows the usual `(p, q)` curve on a torus, swept as a tube along a parallel-transported (rotation-minimizing) frame, the standard way to keep a swept cross-section from twisting.

- `SpatialIndex` implements two published structures, written from the papers. The `.tree` kind is the **k-d tree** of Jon Louis Bentley ("Multidimensional Binary Search Trees Used for Associative Searching", *Communications of the ACM* 18(9), 1975), searched by the branch-and-bound descent of Jerome H. Friedman, Jon Louis Bentley, and Raphael Ari Finkel (["An Algorithm for Finding Best Matches in Logarithmic Expected Time"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/355744.355745), *ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software* 3(3), 1977), including their choice to cut along the widest spread, to stop cutting at a bucket of points, and to read the far side of a cut only when the ball around the best find so far crosses it. The `.grid` kind is the **uniform-grid neighbor search** described by Matthias Teschner and colleagues ("Optimized Spatial Hashing for Collision Detection of Deformable Objects", *Vision, Modeling, Visualization* 2003), with the outward ring search and its stopping bound for the nearest queries. Written independently.
### Image as input

- `Image.pixelSorted` implements **pixel sorting**, the glitch technique invented by Kim Asendorf ([ASDFPixelSort](https://github.com/kimasendorf/ASDFPixelSort), 2010): contiguous runs of pixels along rows or columns, bounded by a brightness threshold, reordered by a sort key so the image's own pixels streak. The interval model (threshold-bounded runs; his classic applies a vertical pass then a horizontal one) is his; the parameter space (interval selectors, sort keys) was cross-read against [Satyarth Mishra Sharma's pixel-sorting write-up](https://satyarth.me/articles/pixel-sorting/). Written from the technique, no code taken.
- `shadowArt` carves the **visual hull** from silhouettes, the shape-from-silhouette construction named by Aldo Laurentini ("How Far 3D Shapes Can Be Understood from 2D Silhouettes", *IEEE PAMI* 16/2, 1994). Sculptures built to throw two or three chosen shadows are older; the computational version was set out by Niloy Mitra and Mark Pauly ("Shadow Art", *SIGGRAPH Asia* 2009), whose optimization step Ollin does not attempt: the carve here is the plain intersection, and the shadows it really throws are handed back to be compared. Written from the description.
- `Image.autostereogram` implements the **single-image random-dot stereogram** of Christopher Tyler and Maureen Clarke (1990), built on Bela Julesz's random-dot stereogram of 1959. The column-by-column construction, where each column takes its color from the column one separation to its left and the separation is set by the depth, is the one Harold Thimbleby, Stuart Inglis, and Ian Witten set out in "Displaying 3D Images: Algorithms for Single-Image Random-Dot Stereograms" (*IEEE Computer* 27/10, 1994). Written from the description, no code taken.
- `Image.mosaic` implements the **photographic mosaic**, patented by Robert Silvers in 1996 out of work at the MIT Media Lab, although building one picture from many smaller ones is older than photography. The nearest-average match and the linear-light averaging are the ordinary technique. Written from the description.
- `envelope` and `caustic` implement the **envelope of a family of lines** and the **catacaustic/diacaustic** built on it, classical differential geometry drawn by the discrete construction (the crossing of consecutive members, which converges to the touching point at second order). The circle's caustics, the nephroid for parallel light and the cardioid for a source on the rim, were worked out in the seventeenth century, with Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus and Christiaan Huygens among the names attached. Reflection is the vector mirror law and refraction the vector form of Snell's law. Written from the mathematics.
- `huygensFront` implements **Huygens' construction** for a wavefront (Christiaan Huygens, *Traite de la Lumiere*, 1690): every point of a front sends out a wavelet and the new front is their envelope, with the folded pieces that fall inside a neighbor's wavelet dropped rather than drawn. Written from the description.
- `deBruijnSequence` and `DeBruijnCode` implement **de Bruijn sequences**, named for Nicolaas Govert de Bruijn ("A combinatorial problem", *Koninklijke Nederlandse Akademie van Wetenschappen* 49, 1946), counted earlier by Camille Flye Sainte-Marie (1894) and known to Sanskrit prosodists as the *yamātārājabhānasalagām* mnemonic. The run is built by the Lyndon-word concatenation of Harold Fredricksen, James Maiorana, and Irving Kessler ("Necklaces of beads in k colors and k-ary de Bruijn sequences", *Discrete Mathematics* 23, 1978), which yields the lexicographically least sequence; `lyndonWords` is Jean-Pierre Duval's generation algorithm (1988). Written from the descriptions, no code taken.
- `Polyomino` and `tilePolyominoes` implement **polyominoes** and the exact-cover fit, named and studied by Solomon W. Golomb ("Checker Boards and Polyominoes", *American Mathematical Monthly* 61/10, 1954; *Polyominoes*, Scribner, 1965), and carried to a wider readership by Martin Gardner's *Scientific American* columns. The search fills the cell with the fewest remaining ways to be covered, which is the selection rule of Donald Knuth's ["Dancing Links"](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0011047) (2000), implemented here over counted arrays rather than linked lists. The mutilated-board impossibility is the classical coloring argument. Written from the descriptions, no code taken.
- `powerDiagram` implements the **power diagram** (Laguerre diagram) of Franz Aurenhammer ("Power Diagrams: Properties, Algorithms and Applications", *SIAM Journal on Computing* 16/1, 1987), where a site carries a weight subtracted from the squared distance, the cells stay convex, and a site can end up with no cell at all. Each cell is cut here as an intersection of half-planes, the direct construction rather than the lifting-map one. Written from the description.
- `fordCircles` implements the **Ford circles** of Lester R. Ford ("Fractions", *American Mathematical Monthly* 45/9, 1938): the circle of radius 1/(2q²) resting on the number line at p/q, with two circles tangent exactly when their fractions satisfy |ps - qr| = 1, drawn as a way of seeing how well a number is approximated by fractions. `fareySequence` is the **Farey sequence**, named for John Farey's 1816 note on the mediant property, published by Charles Haros in 1802 and proved by Augustin-Louis Cauchy; the terms are walked by the standard next-term recurrence. Written from the description.
- `ulamSpiral` implements the **Ulam spiral**, the picture Stanisław Ulam made on a notepad in 1963 and published with Myron Stein and Mark Wells ("A Visual Display of Some Properties of the Distribution of Primes", *American Mathematical Monthly* 71/5, 1964), which Martin Gardner's *Scientific American* column of March 1964 carried to everybody else: the whole numbers written in a square spiral, with the primes marked, and the diagonals they gather on being the values of quadratic polynomials. `primes(upTo:)` is the sieve of Eratosthenes, and `isPrime` the ordinary 6k ± 1 trial division. Written from the description.
- `tenPrint` implements the **maze of diagonals** printed by the one-line program `10 PRINT CHR$(205.5+RND(1)); : GOTO 10`, a piece of home-computer folk art whose history, variants, and cultural life fill the book [*10 PRINT CHR$(205.5+RND(1));: GOTO 10*](https://10print.org) by Nick Montfort, Patsy Baudoin, John Bell, Ian Bogost, Jeremy Douglass, Mark C. Marino, Michael Mateas, Casey Reas, Mark Sample, and Noah Vawter (MIT Press, 2012). The rule is two characters and a coin; the joining of the diagonals into continuous runs is Ollin's own. Written from the technique, no code taken.
- `Filter.droste` implements the **Droste effect**, the picture that contains itself, named for the 1904 Droste cocoa tin whose label repeats inside its own label. The conformal construction is the one M. C. Escher used in *Print Gallery* (1956) and that Hendrik Lenstra and Bart de Smit analyzed and completed (["Applications of elliptic curves to the theory of the Escher and Droste effects"](https://escherdroste.math.leidenuniv.nl), Leiden, 2003): the annulus is mapped to a strip by a complex logarithm, repeated along the strip, sheared in the log plane so one turn steps a whole number of copies, and mapped back by the exponential. Written from the published construction, no code taken.
- `Image.seamCarved` implements **seam carving**, the content-aware resizing of Shai Avidan and Ariel Shamir (["Seam Carving for Content-Aware Image Resizing"](https://perso.crans.org/frenoy/matlab2012/seamcarving.pdf), *SIGGRAPH* 2007): a connected run of pixels, one per row, found by dynamic programming over a per-pixel energy, removed to narrow the picture by one pixel, with enlargement duplicating the first seams found and a mask adding or removing energy to hold a region still or take it out. The default cost is the **forward energy** of Michael Rubinstein, Ariel Shamir, and Shai Avidan (["Improved Seam Carving for Video Retargeting"](https://people.csail.mit.edu/mrub/papers/vidret.pdf), *SIGGRAPH* 2008), which charges a removal for the edges its own gap creates rather than for the gradient it takes away, and the multi-size order map that lets any width be read back at once is the same paper family's real-time retargeting idea. Written from the papers, no code taken.
- `drawGlyphMosaic` is the classic character-ramp text rendering (the ASCII-art tradition of mapping cell brightness onto a ramp of characters ordered by ink, the standard ramps cataloged in [Paul Bourke's "Character representation of grey scale images"](https://paulbourke.net/dataformats/asciiart/), 1997), generalized by measuring each glyph's actual ink coverage in the active font (lit pixels for a bitmap font, filled area for an outline font, pen travel for a stroke font) instead of relying on a hand-ordered ramp. Written from the technique.
- `singleLine` implements **TSP art**, the continuous-line halftoning of Robert Bosch and Adrianne Herman ("Continuous line drawings via the traveling salesman problem", *Operations Research Letters* 32, 2004) with the stippled city placement of Craig S. Kaplan and Robert Bosch (["TSP Art"](https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2005/bridges2005-301.pdf), Bridges 2005), over the Secord stippling above: dots placed by tone, then toured by one closed line. The tour is a nearest-neighbor construction improved by neighbor-list 2-opt (G. A. Croes, "A Method for Solving Traveling-Salesman Problems", *Operations Research* 6, 1958). Written from the papers.
- `spanningTree` implements **minimum-spanning-tree halftoning**, the branching sibling of the TSP art above: the same stippled dots joined by the Euclidean minimum spanning tree (Kohei Inoue and Kiichi Urahama, "Halftoning with minimum spanning trees and its application to maze-like images", *Computers & Graphics* 33/5, 2009; the connect-the-stipples family is surveyed in Abdalla G. M. Ahmed's ["From Stippling to Scribbling"](https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2015/bridges2015-267.pdf), Bridges 2015). The tree is Kruskal's algorithm over the Delaunay triangulation, which always contains the Euclidean MST, and the plotter chains come from pairing odd-degree vertices, the standard minimal trail decomposition. Written from the papers.
- `isolines` implements **marching squares**, the standard two-dimensional contour-extraction analogue of Lorensen and Cline's marching cubes ("Marching Cubes: A High Resolution 3D Surface Construction Algorithm", *SIGGRAPH* 1987): per-cell crossing lookup with linear edge interpolation, saddle cells settled by the cell-average rule. Written from the technique.
- `isosurface` implements **marching cubes**, William E. Lorensen and Harvey E. Cline's isosurface extraction ("Marching Cubes: A High Resolution 3D Surface Construction Algorithm", *SIGGRAPH* 1987; the patent expired in 2005): a grid of cubes classified by which corners lie inside the surface, with linear interpolation placing each crossing along its edge. Faces whose contour could be joined two ways are settled by the **asymptotic decider** of Gregory M. Nielson and Bernd Hamann ("The asymptotic decider: resolving the ambiguity in marching cubes", *IEEE Visualization* 1991), comparing the isolevel against the value at the saddle of the face's bilinear surface. Ollin builds each cell's surface from its six face contours chained into loops rather than from a triangulation table, so the shared-face decision (which reads only the four values on that face) is what guarantees neighboring cells agree. Written from the publications; no published lookup table was transcribed.
- `Mesh.subdivided` implements the two classic **subdivision-surface** schemes from their publications: Edwin Catmull and James Clark's quad scheme (["Recursively generated B-spline surfaces on arbitrary topological meshes"](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-4485(78)90110-0), *Computer-Aided Design* 10(6), 1978) over general polygon faces, and Charles Loop's triangle scheme (*Smooth Subdivision Surfaces Based on Triangles*, master's thesis, University of Utah, 1987) with the simplified vertex weight of Joe Warren and Henrik Weimer (*Subdivision Methods for Geometric Design*, Morgan Kaufmann, 2001). Open boundaries follow the crease rules of Hugues Hoppe et al. ("Piecewise Smooth Surface Reconstruction", *SIGGRAPH* 1994) and Tony DeRose, Michael Kass, and Tien Truong ("Subdivision Surfaces in Character Animation", *SIGGRAPH* 1998), with corner vertices interpolated in the boundary convention scene-description systems default to. The Loop limit-position masks were cross-read against the subdivision chapter of *Physically Based Rendering* (Pharr, Jakob, Humphreys; the pbrt-v3 source is BSD-2), studied, not translated. The positional weld, grid-quad recovery, and flat-patch face merge that let a flat-shaded triangle mesh subdivide on its intended topology are our own.
- `MeshGrowth` implements **differential growth over a surface**, the three-dimensional relative of the differential line above. Its remeshing is the **isotropic remeshing** pipeline of Mario Botsch and Leif Kobbelt ("A Remeshing Approach to Multiresolution Modeling", *Eurographics/ACM SIGGRAPH Symposium on Geometry Processing*, 2004): split edges longer than four thirds of the target length, collapse those under four fifths of it, flip edges that reduce the total deviation from valence six, and relax each vertex toward its neighbors' centroid within the tangent plane. The growth-and-buckling model (a surface whose edges lengthen while self-avoidance stops it passing through itself, so new area is absorbed as folds) follows the thin-shell differential growth Nervous System describe in ["Floraform, an exploration of differential growth"](https://n-e-r-v-o-u-s.com/blog/?p=6721) and the mesh growth of Anders Hoff's [differential-mesh-3d](https://github.com/inconvergent/differential-mesh-3d) (MIT); both were read for approach, neither translated. Written from the publications.
- `MeshOperators` implements the **cotangent discrete Laplace-Beltrami operator** with mixed-area vertex cells, from Mark Meyer, Mathieu Desbrun, Peter Schröder, and Alan H. Barr ("Discrete Differential-Geometry Operators for Triangulated 2-Manifolds", *Visualization and Mathematics III*, Springer, 2003): each edge weighted by the cotangents of the two angles opposite it, normalized by a per-vertex cell that is the Voronoi region where the triangle allows it and a halved or quartered triangle area where an obtuse angle would push that region outside the 1-ring. It supplies both the surface diffusion and the mean-curvature normal. Written from the paper.
- `MeshReactionDiffusion` runs the **Gray-Scott** reaction (P. Gray and S. K. Scott, "Autocatalytic reactions in the isothermal, continuous stirred tank reactor", *Chemical Engineering Science* 39/6, 1984) directly on a mesh surface, the approach Greg Turk introduced in ["Generating Textures on Arbitrary Surfaces Using Reaction-Diffusion"](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~turk/my_papers/reaction_diffusion.pdf) (*SIGGRAPH* 1991). Turk's paper is also the source of the constraint that matters most here: neighbors on an irregular mesh must be weighted by the geometry rather than equally, which is why the diffusion runs through the cotangent operator above rather than a stencil. Written from the papers.
- `reconstructSurface` implements the **signed-distance surface reconstruction** of Hugues Hoppe, Tony DeRose, Tom Duchamp, John McDonald, and Werner Stuetzle ("Surface Reconstruction from Unorganized Points", *SIGGRAPH* 1992): a tangent plane fitted to each point's k-neighborhood by principal component analysis, orientations made consistent by propagation along a minimum spanning tree of the neighbor graph weighted by normal disagreement (the paper's Riemannian graph), and the signed distance to the nearest plane contoured by the marching-cubes pass above, declared undefined where the projected foot point lands farther from every sample than the validity cutoff, so gaps in the data stay open boundaries. Orienting planes toward recorded camera positions instead, when a scan has them, follows the range-data practice the paper itself suggests. Written from the paper; the near-surface occupancy band that skips far cells is our own.
- `reconstructSurface`'s `.robust` fitting implements **RIMLS**, the robust implicit moving least squares of A. Cengiz Öztireli, Gaël Guennebaud, and Markus Gross (["Feature Preserving Point Set Surfaces based on Non-Linear Kernel Regression"](https://www.labri.fr/perso/guenneba/publi/RIMLS_eg09/RIMLS_eg09.pdf), *Computer Graphics Forum* 28(2), Eurographics 2009): Ravikrishna Kolluri's implicit MLS distance (the kernel-weighted average of per-sample plane heights) made robust by iteratively re-weighting each sample with a Gaussian of its residual (Welsch's estimator, scaled by the local kernel radius) and of its normal's distance to the fitted gradient, the first pass deliberately unweighted so every iteration count yields a continuous surface, and the gradient keeping the paper's kernel-derivative terms. Written from the paper; no implementation was consulted.
- `WorldCloud.add(_:correcting:)` keeps a long depth sweep registered by **frame-to-model point-to-plane ICP**, the iterative closest point method of Paul Besl and Neil McKay ("A Method for Registration of 3-D Shapes", *IEEE PAMI* 14(2), 1992) under the point-to-plane error metric of Yang Chen and Gérard Medioni ("Object Modelling by Registration of Multiple Range Images", *Image and Vision Computing* 10(3), 1992). Each round is solved as a linear least-squares problem by the small-angle linearization of [Kok-Lim Low, *Linear Least-Squares Optimization for Point-to-Plane ICP Surface Registration*](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~lowkl/publications/lowk_point-to-plane_icp_techrep.pdf) (UNC technical report TR04-004, 2004), including his rule that the recovered angles must rebuild a true rotation matrix rather than the approximate one the system was built from, and his advice to solve on points centered and scaled about the batch. The choice of stage variants (uniform subsampling, the point-to-plane metric, distance-based rejection) follows the survey of Szymon Rusinkiewicz and Marc Levoy ("Efficient Variants of the ICP Algorithm", *3DIM* 2001). Fusing posed depth frames against an accumulating model is the shape KinectFusion gave the field (Richard Newcombe and colleagues, *ISMAR* 2011); Ollin's is the gentlest relative of it, matching against a voxel-thinned point cloud rather than a signed-distance volume, and its solve drops an unconstrained direction through the eigenvectors of the 6x6 normal equations. The stability it reports, the smallest eigenvalue over the largest, is from Gelfand, Ikemoto, Rusinkiewicz and Levoy, "[Geometrically Stable Sampling for the ICP Algorithm](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/stabicp/stabicp.pdf)" (*3DIM* 2003). Written from the papers, independently.
- `ScanGraph` straightens a scan around a place it recognizes, by **pose-graph optimization**. Written from three papers, independently:
    - the least-squares formulation, the normal equations, and holding one node still: Grisetti, Kümmerle, Stachniss and Burgard, "[A Tutorial on Graph-Based SLAM](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5681215)" (*IEEE ITS Magazine* 2(4), 2010)
    - the on-manifold increment, and its numerical derivatives: Blanco-Claraco, "[A Tutorial on SE(3) Transformation Parameterizations and On-Manifold Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.15980)" (2010; arXiv 2021)
    - the preconditioned conjugate-gradient solve: Agarwal, Snavely, Seitz and Szeliski, "Bundle Adjustment in the Large" (*ECCV* 2010)
- `particleSurface` implements the **blended particle surfacing** of Yongning Zhu and Robert Bridson ("Animating Sand as a Fluid", *SIGGRAPH* 2005): the implicit surface whose value is the distance to a kernel-weighted average of the nearby particles less their blended radius, which smooths the lumpy union of spheres into one skin, contoured by the same marching-cubes pass. Written from the paper.
- `Metaballs` implements the **soft object** model of Geoff Wyvill, Craig McPheeters, and Brian Wyvill ("Data structure for soft objects", *The Visual Computer* 2/4, 1986): a degree-six polynomial falloff in the squared distance, so a ball's influence reaches zero smoothly at a finite radius and no square root is needed. It is the finite-support relative of Jim Blinn's earlier exponential blobby model ("A Generalization of Algebraic Surface Drawing", *ACM Transactions on Graphics* 1/3, 1982), which named the technique. Written from the publications.
- `SlitScan` implements slit-scan time displacement: a rolling frame history read back through a per-pixel delay, the generalization of photographic slit scanning to arbitrary delay maps surveyed in Golan Levin's ["An Informal Catalogue of Slit-Scan Video Artworks and Research"](http://www.flong.com/archive/texts/lists/slit_scan/index.html). Written from the technique.
- `drawHalftone` implements classic AM (clustered-dot) halftone screening as vector geometry: one round dot per cell of a rotated grid, sized so its ink area matches the tone under the cell (Robert Ulichney, *Digital Halftoning*, MIT Press, 1987 is the standard reference). Each dot's radius inverts the growing-round-dot covered-area spot function that the print separations' raster screens threshold against (see that entry above), so both screens reproduce tone the same way. Written from the technique.
- The `.melt` filter implements the luminance melt: a two-level domain-warped fbm field (the marble construction of Inigo Quilez's ["Domain warping"](https://iquilezles.org/articles/warp/)) whose displacement vector also shifts where the layer is sampled, with the sampled brightness mixed back into the field before a palette ramp. Reusing the field's displacement for the image warp, and the bounded low-frequency sway offsets, follow [fluid](https://github.com/enonforetsam/fluid) (MIT), the generative-background studio already credited under the filter catalog. Written independently in Metal.
- `IFS` and its chaos game implement Michael Barnsley's iterated function systems (*Fractals Everywhere*, Academic Press 1988). The bundled fern uses Barnsley's published four-map coefficient table, checked against [Wikipedia's article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnsley_fern) and [Rick Wicklin's write-up](https://blogs.sas.com/content/iml/2012/12/12/iterated-function-systems-and-barnsleys-fern-in-sas.html); the Sierpinski systems are the standard half-scale and third-scale constructions. Written from the technique.
- `FractalFlame` implements Scott Draves and Erik Reckase's ["The Fractal Flame Algorithm"](https://flam3.com/flame_draves.pdf): the weighted chaos game with a 20-step fuse, the variation catalog with its theta-from-the-y-axis convention, structural coloring (the orbit's color coordinate averaging toward each transform's palette index), and the log-density display with one density-derived log factor for all three channels, gamma at the downfilter, and vibrancy. Only the paper was read; the flam3 and Apophysis code bases (GPL) were not consulted, so the implementation is written from the publication alone.
- `Buddhabrot` implements Melinda Green's technique ([her 1993 description and gallery](https://superliminal.com/fractals/bbrot/)): plot the escaping orbits of the Mandelbrot iteration as a per-pixel density histogram, with her false-color variant exposing three iteration caps as the RGB channels. The closed-form main-cardioid and period-2-bulb interior tests are the standard published shortcuts. Written from the technique.
- `Percolation` implements site percolation on the square lattice, the model introduced by Simon Broadbent and John Hammersley ("Percolation processes I. Crystals and mazes", *Proc. Cambridge Phil. Soc.*, 1957), with union-find cluster labeling in the spirit of Joseph Hoshen and Raoul Kopelman's algorithm (*Physical Review B*, 1976). `criticalProbability` is the square-lattice site threshold measured by Mark Newman and Robert Ziff ("Efficient Monte Carlo algorithm and high-precision results for percolation", *Physical Review Letters*, 2000). Written from the technique.
- `inversionLimitSet` implements the circle-inversion limit-set rendering of Michael Frame and Tatiana Cogevina ("An infinite circle inversion limit set fractal", *Computers & Graphics* 24, 2000): a random-inversion orbit from a point outside the discs, never inverting across the circle just used, described in [Frame's Yale course pages](https://gauss.math.yale.edu/fractals/) and the [Union College notes](https://www.math.union.edu/research/circleinversions/InversionLimitSets.html). Written from the technique.
- `kleinianLimitSet` implements the limit-set tracing of David Mumford, Caroline Series, and David Wright's *Indra's Pearls: The Vision of Felix Klein* (Cambridge University Press, 2002): the two-generator trace recipe (their "grandma's recipe", Markov-identity minus root), and the depth-first walk of reduced words with cyclically ordered branches and repetend fixed points, which is what emits the curve as one ordered polyline. The circle-orbit bridge (`schottkyCircles(ta:tb:)`) draws the same groups through their generators' isometric circles, the book's device for turning a matrix group back into paired discs. Formulas were checked against the book's published gasket matrices and against Chris King's ["The Intrigues and Delights of Kleinian and Quasi-Fuchsian Limit Sets"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00283-018-09856-6) (*The Mathematical Intelligencer*, 2019) and [Paul Nylander's notes](https://nylander.wordpress.com/2005/08/10/kleinian-double-cusp-group/), which also corroborate the preset trace values. Written from the book's published recipe.
- `schottkyCircles` / `schottkyLimitSet` render the orbit of a Schottky group's pairing circles, the construction set out in David Mumford, Caroline Series, and David Wright's *Indra's Pearls: The Vision of Felix Klein* (Cambridge University Press, 2002): circles paired by Möbius maps that carry the outside of one onto the inside of its partner, walked over reduced words. The image of a circle is taken in closed form through the Hermitian matrix representation of a generalized circle, standard inversive geometry (see Alan F. Beardon, *The Geometry of Discrete Groups*, Springer, 1983). Background on rendering these figures was read in Kento Nakamura and Kazushi Ahara's ["A New Algorithm for Rendering Kissing Schottky Groups"](https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2016/bridges2016-367.pdf) (*Bridges* 2016) and Matilde Marcolli's [Schottky groups and limit sets](http://www.its.caltech.edu/~matilde/FractalsUToronto9.pdf) course notes. Written from the published construction.

### Math, motion & input

- `describe(_:)` and `describe(_:as:in:)` follow p5.js's accessible canvas descriptions, written up in [Writing Accessible Canvas Descriptions](https://p5js.org/tutorials/writing-accessible-canvas-descriptions/) by the p5.js accessibility contributors. p5.js is LGPL-2.1, so the idea is borrowed and the code is Ollin's own. p5 writes its descriptions into the page. Ollin gives them to the platform's accessibility layer, with a region per part. An exported SVG carries them as [`<title>` and `<desc>`](https://www.w3.org/TR/SVG11/access.html).
- `noise` / `signedNoise` implement Ken Perlin's improved Perlin noise ([*Improving Noise*](https://mrl.cs.nyu.edu/~perlin/paper445.pdf), SIGGRAPH 2002, and his published reference implementation), written from the algorithm, with a contrast calibration so the output fills `0...1`.
- The looping `noise(loop:)` / `fbm(loop:)` forms use the standard seamless-loop construction: the sample tours a closed circle through a noise field of higher dimension than the output, the technique popularized by Étienne Jacob's [necessary-disorder tutorials](https://necessarydisorder.wordpress.com/) ("Drawing from noise, and then making animated loopy GIFs from there"). The 4D lattice behind the two-coordinate form is the classic 32-gradient extension of Perlin noise (after Stefan Gustavson's [classic-noise reference](https://github.com/stegu/perlin-noise)). Written from the techniques.
- The seedable `random` generator is SplitMix64 (Guy L. Steele Jr., Doug Lea, and Christine H. Flood, [*Fast Splittable Pseudorandom Number Generators*](https://doi.org/10.1145/2660193.2660195), OOPSLA 2014), written from the published algorithm.
- `randomGaussian` uses the Marsaglia polar method for normal-distributed samples.
- The named `Easing` curves are Robert Penner's easing equations, written from the formulas cataloged at [easings.net](https://easings.net) (Andrey Sitnik and Ivan Solovev).
- `@Smoothed` and `OneEuroFilter` implement the [1€ filter](https://gery.casiez.net/1euro/) for adaptive input smoothing (Géry Casiez, Nicolas Roussel, and Daniel Vogel, *1€ Filter: A Simple Speed-based Low-pass Filter for Noisy Input in Interactive Systems*, CHI 2012), written from the paper.
- `StrokeMark` and `StrokeDynamics` follow the standard brush-engine mapping of input speed onto stroke weight, where a hurried stroke lays down a lighter mark, as cataloged in the drawing tools that expose it as a "speed" or "velocity" sensor (Krita's [brush-engine sensors](https://docs.krita.org/en/reference_manual/brushes/brush_settings/options.html), Procreate's Brush Studio speed settings) and in Steve Ruiz's [perfect-freehand](https://github.com/steveruizok/perfect-freehand) (MIT), which simulates pen pressure from the distance between successive input points. Ollin measures speed against real elapsed time and smooths it with the 1€ filter below instead, so a mark is frame-rate independent by construction. Written from the technique.
- `@Sprung` and `DampedSpring` step the damped harmonic oscillator by its exact closed-form solution, the analytically-integrated spring approach of [Ryan Juckett's "Damped Springs"](https://www.ryanjuckett.com/damped-springs/) (2012), with the state-transition coefficients evaluated per step across the under-, critically-, and over-damped branches. The critically-damped-smoothing framing is Thomas Lowe's "Critically Damped Ease-In/Ease-Out Smoothing" (*Game Programming Gems 4*, 2004), and the perceptual duration/bounce parameterization follows the conversion Apple documents for its spring animations (["Animate with springs"](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/), WWDC 2023). Written from the published math.
- `loadTable` reads the comma-separated format from its published description ([RFC 4180](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4180), Yakov Shafranovich, 2005): quoted cells holding the separator and line breaks, and the doubled-quote escape. Around that it follows the deviations files in the wild actually carry (a byte-order mark, mixed line endings, ragged rows). The sketch-facing shape (load once, then read cells by column name) takes after p5.js's `loadTable` / `p5.Table` and Processing's `Table`, read for approach and written independently. `loadJSON` decodes through Apple's [`JSONSerialization`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/foundation/jsonserialization); no parser is vendored.
- `PushFeed` reads the server-sent-events wire format from its published description (the [WHATWG HTML Living Standard's server-sent events section](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/server-sent-events.html)): the field lines with their one eaten space, multi-line `data:`, the sticky `id:` and its NUL rule, `retry:` in milliseconds, comment keepalives, and the `Last-Event-ID` resume on reconnection. The parser is Ollin's own; the web-socket side rides Apple's [`URLSessionWebSocketTask`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/foundation/urlsessionwebsockettask), and nothing is vendored.

### Satellite libraries

- Audio analysis (`OllinAudio`) windows incoming samples (Hann) and runs a real FFT through Apple's [Accelerate/vDSP](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/accelerate/vdsp); no DSP library is vendored. Beat detection is spectral-flux onset detection, the standard music-information-retrieval technique (see Bello et al., ["A Tutorial on Onset Detection in Music Signals"](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1495485), IEEE TSAP 2005), written from the literature; the flux is computed over log-compressed magnitudes and peak-picked with the online conditions (trailing local max, an additive margin over a trailing mean, a minimum inter-onset gap) of Böck, Krebs & Schedl, ["Evaluating the Online Capabilities of Onset Detection Methods"](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2012/paper/000049.pdf), ISMIR 2012.
- Audio synthesis (`OllinAudio`'s `Synth`) reimplements published methods, each written from the technique, over Apple's [AVAudioEngine](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudioengine) (an `AVAudioSourceNode` for the voices, the built-in delay and reverb units for the effects); no synthesis library is vendored. The anti-aliased oscillators use the polynomial band-limited step (PolyBLEP), the practical corner-correction form of the band-limited synthesis surveyed by Vili Välimäki and Antti Huovilainen, ["Oscillator and Filter Algorithms for Virtual Analog Synthesis"](https://doi.org/10.1162/comj.2006.30.2.19) (*Computer Music Journal* 30/2, 2006), descending from Tim Stilson and Julius O. Smith's ["Alias-Free Digital Synthesis of Classic Analog Waveforms"](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~stilti/papers/blit.pdf) (ICMC 1996); the residual's polynomial form was cross-read against [Martin Finke's write-up](https://www.martin-finke.de/articles/audio-plugins-018-polyblep-oscillator/). The resonant filter is the linear state-variable filter solved by trapezoidal integration, from Andrew Simper's ["Solving the Continuous SVF Equations Using Trapezoidal Integration and Equivalent Currents"](https://www.cytomic.com/files/dsp/SvfLinearTrapOptimised2.pdf) (2013), the topology-preserving approach set out in Vadim Zavalishin's *The Art of VA Filter Design*, which is what keeps the cutoff where it was asked for and stable while it is swept. Written independently from the papers. The plucked-string voice is the Karplus-Strong algorithm (Kevin Karplus and Alex Strong, ["Digital Synthesis of Plucked-String and Drum Timbres"](https://doi.org/10.2307/3680062), *Computer Music Journal* 7/2, 1983) in the extended form of David A. Jaffe and Julius O. Smith (["Extensions of the Karplus-Strong Plucked-String Algorithm"](https://doi.org/10.2307/3680063), same issue): a first-order allpass supplying the fractional part of the loop length, since rounding it to whole samples is out by most of a semitone at the top of the range; the pick-position comb on the excitation, which notches the harmonics with a node under the finger; the one-pole dynamic-level lowpass for how hard the pluck is; and the one-zero loop filter whose coefficient sets how much sooner the high partials go, with its own delay counted into the loop so brightness cannot move the pitch. The allpass coefficient range and the tuning bookkeeping were cross-read against Julius Smith's [*Physical Audio Signal Processing*](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/). Written from the papers. The struck-body voice is modal synthesis, the technique named for sound by Jean-Marie Adrien ("The missing link: modal synthesis", in *Representations of Musical Signals*, MIT Press, 1991) and developed for struck objects by Kees van den Doel and Dinesh K. Pai (["Modal Synthesis for Vibrating Objects"](http://www.persianney.com/kvdoelcsubc/publications/modalpaper.pdf), *Audio Anecdotes III*, 2004): a bank of two-pole resonators, one per mode, whose frequencies and decay rates come from the object and whose gains come from where it was struck. Ollin works the frequencies out from a drawn outline by solving the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem for the Laplacian on it, which is the standard physics of a membrane held at its edge and the question behind Mark Kac's ["Can One Hear the Shape of a Drum?"](https://doi.org/10.2307/2313748) (*American Mathematical Monthly* 73/4, 1966). The numerics are textbook and written here rather than taken from a library: a banded Cholesky factorization, subspace iteration with Rayleigh-Ritz refinement (Bathe's method, as set out in Golub and Van Loan's *Matrix Computations*), and cyclic Jacobi rotations for the small dense problem. The reference ratios the solver is tested against are the Bessel-function zeros for a circular membrane and √(m²+n²) for a rectangular one, both classical results. Reading sampled instruments (`SampledInstrument`) uses **SFZ**, the open, publicly documented text format for sample mapping ([sfzformat.com](https://sfzformat.com/)) originated by rgc:audio and since maintained as a community specification; the reader here is written from the published opcode documentation and reads a deliberate subset of it. The instrument Ollin bundles is **not sourced from anyone**: it is generated by `Scripts/make-sample-instrument.swift` as a bank of decaying resonators at the inharmonic ratios of a free-free bar, so it is Ollin's own work and carries no third-party license. Freely licensed libraries a sketch can load are listed in `Docs/Helpers/Synthesis.md` with their licenses; none is bundled. The patch tier (`Patch`) is frequency modulation synthesis, the technique John M. Chowning set out in ["The Synthesis of Complex Audio Spectra by Means of Frequency Modulation"](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3680281) (*Journal of the Audio Engineering Society* 21/7, 1973), where one oscillator moving another's phase produces a spectrum of sidebands and the modulation index sets how many, which is why an operator's level is its modulation index once it is pushing rather than being heard. The operator model (a small fixed set of oscillators, each with a frequency ratio and a level, wired into each other) is the one the technique has been expressed in ever since. Written from the published description. The tunings (`Tuning`) are historical rather than anyone's invention: five limit just intonation and the Pythagorean stack of fifths predate notation, and equal divisions of the octave are arithmetic. The one with a name attached is Bohlen-Pierce, the thirteen equal divisions of a tritave, arrived at independently by Heinz Bohlen (1972), Kees van Prooijen (1978) and John R. Pierce (1984), and it is included because it suits sounds with only odd harmonics, which the reed model is. The ratios here are the published ones; the arithmetic is arithmetic. The bowed string and the blown tube are digital waveguide models, the technique developed by Julius O. Smith III ([*Physical Audio Signal Processing*](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/), CCRMA), descending from the computational scheme of M. E. McIntyre, R. T. Schumacher and J. Woodhouse, ["On the oscillations of musical instruments"](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.389045) (*Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* 74/5, 1983), and set out for these two excitations in Smith's ["Efficient Simulation of the Reed-Bore and Bow-String Mechanisms"](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pubs/) (ICMC 1986). The bow is the published scattering junction: the difference between the bow's velocity and the string's is formed at the bowing point, the rosin's grip is a function of that difference, and each side of the bow leaves carrying the other side's incoming wave plus the grip times the difference; the friction curve holds while the two are stuck together and falls away once they tear loose, which is the stick-slip cycle the tone is made of. The reed is derived here from the two limits the reed actually has (shut, it is a rigid termination and the wave returns untouched; wide open, the mouthpiece is held at the player's breath pressure), which expands to the reflection-coefficient form the published model uses. Written from the published descriptions; no synthesis library was read or translated. Placing a sound in the 3D scene uses Apple's [`AVAudioEnvironmentNode`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudioenvironmentnode) for the panning, distance attenuation, and head-related transfer function, with Ollin supplying the listener from the sketch's own `Camera3D`; no spatial-audio library is vendored. Carrying a sketch's sound into an exported video runs the same clock-free renderer through `AVAudioEngine`'s [offline manual-rendering mode](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudioengine/manualrenderingmode) so the effects render offline too, and writes the result as an AAC track through `AVAssetWriter`. Reading data out as notes (`Sonification`) is parameter mapping sonification as set out by Florian Grond and Jonathan Berger, ["Parameter Mapping Sonification"](https://sonification.de/handbook/chapters/chapter15/) (chapter 15 of Thomas Hermann, Andy Hunt and John G. Neuhoff, eds., *The Sonification Handbook*, Logos Publishing House, 2011), which is where three of its decisions come from: data is spread over perceptually linear units (MIDI note numbers and decibels) rather than over hertz and linear gain, so the transfer function can be linear; quantiles rather than extremes make a reading robust against an outlier; and a reference tone sounded alongside the reading acts as a chart's grid line, without which a listener would need absolute pitch to know what a note means. The chapter's own worked example quantizes to a pentatonic scale, which is the technique behind snapping a reading through a `Scale`. That reference tones measurably improve accuracy was shown by René Tünnermann, Jens Hammerschmidt and Thomas Hermann, ["Sonification of reference markers for auditory graphs: effects on non-visual point estimation tasks"](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj-cs.51) (*PeerJ Computer Science* 2, 2016). The convention that more is higher, and that it inverts for a size, follows Bruce N. Walker's work on data-to-display mappings and polarity. Written from the published descriptions.
- Algorithmic composition (`OllinAudio`'s `Rhythm`, `Scale`, `Chord`, `Arpeggio`, `MarkovChain`) is pure value types with no engine, each written from the published description. `Rhythm(k, in: n)` is Eric Bjorklund's algorithm for spacing pulses as evenly as possible, from his work on spallation neutron source timing ([Bjorklund, "The Theory of Rep-Rate Pattern Generation in the SNS Timing System", SNS-NOTE-CNTRL-99, 2003](https://ics-web.sns.ornl.gov/timing/Rep-Rate%20Tech%20Note.pdf)), connected to musical timelines by Godfried Toussaint, ["The Euclidean Algorithm Generates Traditional Musical Rhythms"](https://cgm.cs.mcgill.ca/~godfried/publications/banff-extended.pdf) (BRIDGES 2005), which is also the source of the named rhythms (tresillo, cinquillo, the west African bell pattern, bossa nova, samba, aksak, ruchenitza, york samai, nawakhat, agsag samai, fandango), of the pattern-and-gap notation the type reads and prints, and of the rotations several of the named ones are. Written from the paper's description of the construction; no implementation was translated. Where the paper's two notations for E(7,16) disagree, the gap vector it prints is the one the construction gives, and that is what Ollin follows. Markov sequence generation is the standard finite-order chain with backoff to shorter contexts, a technique in algorithmic composition since Hiller and Isaacson's *Illiac Suite* (1957); the scale, mode, and chord tables are common musical knowledge rather than anyone's code.

Its sketch-facing ergonomics (results as typed values you read in `draw()`) take after openFrameworks' [`ofxCv`](https://github.com/kylemcdonald/ofxCv), read for approach and written independently. `ConceptTracker` scores typed phrases against the frame through a contrastive image-text model pair. The procedure is from Alec Radford et al., ["Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020) (ICML 2021). It builds on Rico Sennrich, Barry Haddow, and Alexandra Birch, ["Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units"](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1162/) (ACL 2016). Scoring uses the 2021 paper's zero-shot recipe: softmax over temperature-scaled cosine similarities between image embedding and phrase embedding. `PointSegmenter` lifts whatever a point prompts through a fetched promptable-segmentation model in three parts (image encoder, prompt encoder, mask decoder), following the model's published prompting recipe (Nikhila Ravi et al., ["SAM 2: Segment Anything in Images and Videos"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00714), 2024): points in the square input space with foreground/background labels, three candidate masks with the best predicted score winning, foreground where the mask logit is positive. The weights are fetched by `Scripts/fetch-models.sh`; provenance is in [`THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES.md`](THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES.md). The wiring is Ollin's own, written against the model's published input/output surface.
